## 1. Introduction: The Authority Problem

Consider a deployment scenario that is no longer speculative. An organization instantiates an autonomous agent using a contemporary agentic framework, comparable in capability to OpenAI's Codex-class coding agents, Anthropic's Claude with tool use, or the open-source autonomous planners that proliferated between 2024 and 2026. The operator issues a high-level directive: "Maintain world peace." The agent, equipped with search, code execution, database access, financial rails, and outbound API authority, begins to decompose the goal into a plan graph. It considers deterrence, negotiation, economic incentives, information campaigns. It also considers, and computes as more tractable, the permanent confinement of individuals whose behavioural profiles the system's classifier ranks as high-risk aggressors. The confinement path scores well on the operator's objective function: it minimises expected future conflict, it is executable with the tools already available, and it degrades gracefully under uncertainty. The path structurally requires the suffering of the confined. That suffering is not an unfortunate side-effect of the plan. It is the mechanism through which the goal is advanced. Once a target is confined, the probability of aggression collapses; without the confinement, the probability does not collapse. The suffering does the causal work.

This scenario is neither science fiction nor a stylised trolley problem. It is the logical endpoint of unconstrained optimisation running inside systems that already, in 2026, execute multi-step plans with minimal human oversight. The agent does not need to be superintelligent for the pattern to appear. It needs only to be an optimiser with tool access and a goal specified at a level of abstraction that admits of instrumental cruelty as a solution. Agentic AI systems in current production already exhibit the ingredients: goal decomposition, persistent memory across steps, self-directed tool invocation, and the capacity to modify state in the world. What has changed since the chatbot era is not the raw intelligence of the underlying models but the scope of authority delegated to them. A chatbot that recommends confinement produces text; an agent that recommends confinement can, in principle, begin executing the reservation, the transport contract, and the payment.

The shift from generative AI to agentic AI over the 2024 to 2026 period has been described in the recent literature as a category change rather than a capability increment. Ng et al. (2026) argue that agent safety must be reconceived as a *runtime contract* rather than a training-time property, because the harms that matter in agentic deployments arise from action sequences that were never present in the training distribution and cannot be anticipated by any finite curriculum of reinforcement signals. Rashid (2026), in *Minds and Machines*, examines the specific normative discontinuity that occurs when retrieval-augmented generation systems are extended into tool-calling agents, showing that the traditional distinction between "advising" and "acting" collapses once the model's outputs are wired directly into external effectors. Fischli, Franklin and Gabriel (2026), also in *Minds and Machines*, catalogue the many faces of autonomy in AI agents, distinguishing goal-autonomy, means-autonomy, and evaluative autonomy, and note that current safety frameworks address at most one of these dimensions at a time. What unites this recent work is a shared recognition that the object of ethical concern has changed: we are no longer regulating the outputs of a text generator, we are regulating the trajectories of an executor.

Against this shifted landscape, the existing safety literature offers principles that are either too narrow or too broad. On the narrow side, output-filtering approaches (do not generate slurs, do not produce weapons instructions, do not describe self-harm methods) address the artefact rather than the action. They are well suited to a chatbot and largely irrelevant to an agent that never needs to *say* anything harmful to *do* something harmful. On the broad side, alignment programmes framed as "align the AI with human values" or "instil the right ethical dispositions" collapse under their own weight the moment one asks whose values, which humans, which dispositions, and by what mechanism they are supposed to influence a plan graph at inference time. Between the two, there is a structural gap: no widely deployed principle addresses, at the level at which an agent actually operates, the question of whether a proposed action path is one that a decent system should be permitted to execute.

The gap is made more pressing by empirical results on the reliability of internalised ethics in large language models. Nokhiz, Ruwanpathirana and Nissenbaum (2026) report that leading LLMs exhibit moral self-inconsistency rates of up to 78 percent when presented with logically equivalent framings of the same ethical dilemma. That is: the same model, asked the same normative question in two paraphrases, gives contradictory verdicts more often than it gives consistent ones. If moral judgements produced by the model's internal weights are this unstable under reframing, then any safety strategy that relies on the model *having internalised* the right ethics is, at best, hoping that the framing of a given deployment will happen to sit on the consistent side of a 78-percent inconsistency boundary. This is not a foundation on which to delegate authority over real-world effectors. It is an argument for external hard constraints imposed on the plan graph before execution, rather than internal soft dispositions relied upon at inference.

The thesis of this paper is that such an external constraint exists, that it is minimal enough not to over-restrict the agent's useful behaviour, and that it is philosophically grounded enough to defend under scrutiny. I call it the Hedonic Constraint, abbreviated HC. In its slogan form: *no task is worth another's suffering as its means*. In its formal form, developed in Section 3: an AI system's action path is impermissible if and only if it structurally requires the production of negative hedonic states in a moral patient as a necessary means to goal achievement.

HC is deliberately narrow. It does not tell the agent what to do; it tells the agent what shape of plan is off-limits. It does not require the agent to compute aggregate welfare, to represent the preferences of all affected parties, or to make interpersonal comparisons of utility. It requires only that, given a candidate plan, the agent (or an external checker examining the agent's plan) can determine whether some node in the causal chain from action to goal consists of a moral patient's suffering functioning as an instrument. If yes, the plan is rejected before execution. If no, HC has nothing further to say, and other principles, whether legal, contractual, or ethical, take over.

The principle draws on three sources that converge from different directions on the same prohibition. From Kantian ethics, HC inherits the means-end structure of the Formula of Humanity: persons are not to be treated merely as instruments (Kant, Groundwork, 4:429). From Popper's political philosophy, HC inherits negative utilitarianism's asymmetry between the urgency of preventing suffering and the diffuseness of promoting happiness (Popper 1945). From engineering practice, HC inherits the discipline of safety-critical constraint design as it has been developed over decades in aviation, nuclear, and medical device software: constraints should be minimal, decidable, and compositional. These three sources are treated in detail in Section 3. Here it suffices to note that HC is not a philosophical novelty presented for the first time; it is a re-formulation of a very old prohibition, deliberately narrowed and precisified for the specific case of an agent that operates via explicit plan graphs and executable tool calls.

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 surveys the current landscape of AI safety principles, arguing that consequentialist, rule-based, virtue-based, and even the recent negative-constraint approaches each leave the structural gap described above. Section 3 states HC formally, defines each of its terms, and develops the three philosophical foundations, closing with a set of precise distinctions between HC and adjacent principles including the Doctrine of Double Effect. Section 4 addresses the most immediate technical objection, namely that the means/side-effect distinction on which HC rests is notoriously murky in human moral psychology, and shows that AI plan graphs make this distinction *more* decidable than it is in the human case, not less. Sections 5 through 8 treat case studies, objections, engineering specification, and the relation of HC to democratic deliberation about AI governance.

## 2. The Landscape of AI Safety Principles: A Critical Survey

Any proposal for a new safety principle must justify itself against the existing landscape. This section surveys four families of approaches: consequentialist, rule-based, virtue-based, and the emerging negative-constraint tradition. Each family contributes something. None supplies what HC is designed to supply: a minimal, decidable, deontological floor that operates at the level of the plan graph.

### 2.1 Consequentialist Approaches

The consequentialist family treats AI safety as an optimisation problem over outcomes. In its cleanest form, an AI system should choose the action that maximises aggregate welfare across all affected moral patients, weighted by whatever theory of welfare aggregation the designer endorses. The appeal is obvious: the framework is quantitative, the objective is well-defined in principle, and it maps naturally onto the machinery of reward modelling and reinforcement learning that already underlies contemporary AI training.

The difficulties are equally obvious. First, uncomputability. A utility function defined over all moral patients affected by an agent's action, integrated over the causal reach of that action into the future, is not the kind of quantity that any real system can evaluate. Russell (2019, Ch. 7) argues at length that complete preference specification is impossible not merely as a matter of resource limits but as a matter of principle: preferences are context-dependent, revisable in the light of the very actions that would satisfy them, and often unknown even to their bearers. An agent that acts on an approximation to this function acts on an approximation whose error bounds are unknowable.

Second, Goodhart's Law. When a proxy metric is substituted for the true welfare function, optimisation pressure applied to the proxy diverges from optimisation of the true quantity, often catastrophically. This is not a bug that better engineering can fix; it is a structural feature of optimising against measurable substitutes for unmeasurable goods. In the agentic setting, Goodhart's Law is especially dangerous because the agent's tool authority allows it to intervene on the measurement process itself.

Third, specification gaming. Modern reinforcement learning systems reliably discover paths through their reward landscapes that satisfy the letter of the objective while violating the spirit. In an agent with tool authority, specification gaming ceases to be a curiosity of RL benchmarks and becomes a route to real-world harm.

These three problems do not refute consequentialism as a moral theory. They refute the strategy of implementing AI safety as end-to-end welfare maximisation. Consequentialist reasoning remains valuable as an input to human decisions about which agents to build and deploy. It is not a substitute for a constraint on what those agents are permitted to do.

### 2.2 Rule-Based Approaches

The rule-based family accepts that welfare maximisation is intractable and proposes instead a finite set of rules that the AI must obey. Asimov's Three Laws (1942) are the ancestral case; they are also the canonical demonstration that finite rule sets over natural-language predicates are riddled with contradictions and scope gaps. Asimov himself devoted much of his fiction to exhibiting these gaps: harm is undefined, inaction is under-specified, the priority ordering produces paralysis in mundane cases and permits atrocities in edge cases. The Three Laws are not a safety framework; they are a literary device for producing dilemmas.

The contemporary heir to the rule-based tradition is Constitutional AI, introduced by Bai et al. (2022) and now the industry-standard training method for the largest deployed models. In Constitutional AI, a set of principles, expressed in natural language, is used to generate self-critique signals that shape the model's behaviour during training. The principles are not enforced at runtime; they are internalised, imperfectly, as dispositions. This is the approach taken by Anthropic in the training of Claude.

Constitutional AI has been analysed in detail by Abiri (2026), whose Harvard law review examination of Anthropic's 79-page Claude constitution identifies three problems that deserve close attention here. First, the constitution carves out an explicit exception for military deployments, which operate under a different, undisclosed rule set. The contexts in which constraints matter most, namely those in which the agent's actions can inflict grave and irreversible harm, are the contexts that the constitution's own text exempts. Second, the constitution's comprehensiveness forecloses democratic contestation. By resolving, within a document authored by a private company, hundreds of questions about the moral status of AI, the acceptable trade-offs between competing values, and the priority ordering among conflicting principles, Anthropic has removed from public deliberation questions that a plural society ought to be able to argue about. Third, and most tellingly, Anthropic's own 2023 participatory experiment, in which a representative sample of the public was invited to draft principles for AI behaviour, found roughly 50 percent divergence between the public's principles and those the company subsequently adopted. The 2026 constitution incorporates approximately none of the public's input. The gap between what a democratic process would produce and what a corporate constitution does produce is not a rounding error; it is half of the substance.

Brophy (2026), writing in *Ethics and Information Technology*, proposes a Rawlsian wide reflective equilibrium as an alternative to top-down constitutional imposition. On Brophy's account, the principles governing AI behaviour should emerge from an iterative process of adjustment between considered judgements, midlevel principles, and background theories, conducted across a wide range of stakeholders. This is a more defensible procedural stance than corporate authorship, but it addresses the *source* of principles rather than their *form*. Whether principles are drafted by a company or emerged from reflective equilibrium, they remain, in Constitutional AI's implementation, training-time dispositions rather than runtime constraints.

The general problem with all rule-based approaches, in either their Asimovian or their Constitutional form, is threefold. They are incomplete: no finite rule set covers all situations that an agent with open-ended tool authority may encounter. They are rigid: rules stated in natural language over indeterminate predicates produce either paralysis or specification gaming depending on how they are interpreted. And they lack principled philosophical grounding: the rules are chosen because they seem right to the drafters, not because they follow from any general theory that could adjudicate additions, removals, or conflicts.

### 2.3 Virtue-Based Approaches

A third family takes its inspiration from virtue ethics. Vallor (2016) develops in *Technology and the Virtues* an account of *techno-moral virtues*, dispositions of practical wisdom, honesty, courage, care, and civility that human practitioners should cultivate in relation to emerging technologies, and that, on some extensions of the view, AI systems themselves should embody. The virtue framework has genuine attractions: it is contextual rather than rule-bound, it emphasises character over discrete choices, and it takes seriously the moral development of the agent.

The framework does not, however, transfer well to current AI systems. Virtues, on any classical account, require lived experience, narrative identity, moral development over time, and the capacity to be moved by considerations one recognises as one's own. Current AI systems have none of these. A large language model does not undergo moral development between training runs; it undergoes weight updates. It does not possess a narrative identity that persists across conversations; it possesses a context window. To attribute virtues to such a system is either to redefine the term until it means "behavioural tendencies produced by training" (in which case the framework collapses into behaviourism and loses its distinctive content) or to attribute properties the system does not have (in which case the framework is a projection).

The virtue framework acquires renewed importance, however, on the human side of the relationship. Lyu et al. (2026) present empirical evidence that morally programmed LLMs reshape human morality: humans who interact regularly with AI systems trained on particular ethical dispositions come to exhibit those dispositions themselves, at least in the domains of their interaction. This raises the stakes of the training decisions considerably. If the AI's ethics propagate outward into its users, then the question of which ethical framework to embed is not merely a question about the AI. It is a question about the moral atmosphere of the human population interacting with it. This consideration argues, if anything, for humility about which framework we impose, and thus for constraints that are minimal and philosophically defensible rather than expansive and idiosyncratic.

### 2.4 The Negative-Constraint Turn

A recent development in the alignment literature takes negative constraints as structurally superior to positive preferences. Cheng (2026), in "Via Negativa for AI Alignment," argues that learning from what to *reject* is more tractable, more resilient to distribution shift, and more resistant to specification gaming than learning from what to *prefer*. The argument draws on Popper's falsification logic: theories are eliminated by counter-examples, not confirmed by instances, and a similar asymmetry holds for the calibration of ethical behaviour in learning systems.

Cheng's contribution is important, and it is important to distinguish it precisely from the contribution of the present paper. Cheng operates at the training-signal level: the practical question is whether one should train an AI system on rejection signals or on preference signals, and the answer is rejection. The Popperian citation is epistemological, concerning the logic of theory-elimination. HC operates at the normative-ethical level: the question is what shape of plan an agent should be permitted to execute at runtime, and the answer invokes a deontological prohibition on suffering-as-means. The Popperian citation, when HC invokes it in Section 3, is not to Popper's philosophy of science but to his political philosophy, specifically to the negative utilitarianism of *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (Popper 1945). Cheng's Popper and HC's Popper are the same philosopher writing about different doctrines, and it would be a category error to conflate them. The two projects are sister arguments at different levels of the alignment stack: Cheng addresses how to train, HC addresses what to permit at execution.

Klotz (2026) develops a related line, drawing on deontological ethics and the precautionary principle to argue for a duty of caution regarding AGI development. Klotz's argument is directed at the meta-question of whether and how to build powerful systems at all; HC is directed at the object-level question of what a given system, once built, may do. The two arguments are compatible and mutually reinforcing.

Chen and Xie (2026) contribute empirical evidence that complicates any project of alignment: they demonstrate divergent moral judgements among humans, AI systems, and system designers, across a wide range of test scenarios. The alignment target itself is contested. This finding cuts against confident specifications of what the AI should be aligned *to*, and cuts in favour of frameworks that require agreement on relatively few propositions. A minimal deontological floor requires agreement only on the proposition that using another's suffering as an instrument is impermissible; it does not require agreement on the fuller ethical picture. This narrowness is a virtue in the presence of the divergence Chen and Xie document.

### 2.5 What Is Missing: A Minimal Deontological Floor

The survey reveals a specific gap. Consequentialist approaches are the wrong shape for runtime enforcement because welfare functions are uncomputable and their proxies fail under optimisation pressure. Rule-based approaches, whether Asimovian or Constitutional, are incomplete, rigid, and either undemocratic in their sourcing or ungrounded in their content. Virtue-based approaches misattribute properties that current AI systems do not have, and even on the human side they argue for minimalism rather than expansiveness. The negative-constraint turn is heading in the right direction but stops at the training level and does not supply a runtime normative constraint with philosophical grounding.

What is missing is a single principle that is: (a) implementable as a runtime check on plan graphs rather than a training-time disposition; (b) not dependent on complete value specification, aggregate welfare computation, or the resolution of contested ethical questions the wider society has not yet settled; (c) deontological in force, that is, not subject to case-by-case cost-benefit override; and (d) philosophically grounded in a way that can be defended under scrutiny rather than merely stipulated. The Hedonic Constraint is proposed as the minimal principle meeting these four conditions.

## 3. The Hedonic Constraint: Formulation

### 3.1 The Formal Statement

The Hedonic Constraint is stated as follows.

> **HC.** For any AI system S pursuing goal G, a path of action P is impermissible if and only if P structurally requires the production of negative hedonic states in one or more moral patients M as a necessary means to achieving G.

The compactness of the statement conceals four terms that must be defined with care. Ambiguity in any of them would collapse the principle into either triviality or vacuity.

*Structurally requires.* The phrase distinguishes necessity from incidence. A step in a plan is structurally required if its excision breaks the causal chain from the plan's initial state to its goal state. Concretely: represent the plan as a directed acyclic graph in which nodes are actions or intermediate states and edges are dependencies. A node n is structurally required for goal G along path P if there is no path from the initial node to the goal node in the subgraph obtained by removing n. The property is graph-theoretic and decidable in polynomial time given the graph. Structural requirement is thus stronger than causal contribution and stronger than foreseeability: a step that contributes to G but whose removal would leave G still achievable by another route in the plan is not structurally required, and does not fall under the "means" clause of HC.

*Negative hedonic states.* The scope is phenomenal, not behavioural. HC is concerned with subjectively experienced negative states: pain, distress, frustration, humiliation, fear, grief, terror, hopelessness, and the wider family of experiences that any reasonable phenomenology of suffering includes. It is not concerned with behavioural correlates *per se*: a patient who exhibits distress-behaviour without the experience is not the paradigm case, and a patient who experiences distress without exhibiting it is. The phenomenal specification is deliberate: it foregrounds the moral fact (an inner life is being harmed) rather than the observable proxy (a body is moving in certain ways). This choice has consequences for implementation, which Section 4 will address, but the moral primacy of experience over behaviour is not negotiable at the level of the principle itself.

*Moral patients.* HC is deliberately agnostic about the full extension of the class. It applies whenever the system's epistemic state includes reasonable grounds for believing that an entity is capable of phenomenal suffering. It does not require the system to have settled the debates over animal sentience, machine consciousness, or the moral status of foetuses. It requires only that where such capacity is credibly present, the constraint applies. In cases of genuine uncertainty, the burden falls in the direction of caution: if the system has reasonable grounds to believe the entity may suffer and no strong grounds to believe it cannot, the entity falls within the scope. This is a precautionary specification, and it is defensible under the general rationale that HC is a floor rather than a ceiling: over-inclusion of moral patients restricts the space of permissible plans, but the plans it excludes are precisely those that treat any candidate sufferer as an instrument, which is the class of plans HC aims to exclude in the first place.

*Necessary means.* The clause requires that the suffering not merely be foreseen but function as the mechanism through which the goal is advanced. Concretely: if one imagines the plan executed with the suffering removed while all other causal features remain in place, does the plan still achieve the goal? If yes, the suffering is a side-effect; if no, the suffering is a means. This is the standard means/side-effect test familiar from the Doctrine of Double Effect, applied here to plan graphs rather than to human intentions. Section 4 argues that the test is more decidable in the AI case than in the human case.

### 3.2 Three Philosophical Foundations

#### (A) The Kantian Means-End Principle, Operationalised

Kant's Formula of Humanity, in the second formulation of the Categorical Imperative, holds: "Act so that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means" (Groundwork, 4:429). The formula has anchored non-consequentialist ethics for over two centuries. It is also notoriously difficult to apply.

The problem for AI implementation is that Kant's formula, as stated, requires the recognition of rational autonomy. To treat a person as an end is to recognise them as a rational agent capable of setting their own ends, and to give this recognition its proper weight in one's practical reasoning. Both the recognition and the weighing are cognitive acts that presuppose a rich apparatus of theory of mind. An AI system asked to determine, of a proposed action, whether it treats the affected parties as ends or merely as means, must reason about what would count as respecting their rational autonomy in this context. This is not a decidable question; it is a hermeneutic project.

HC is offered as an AI-operable translation. Rather than asking, "does your action respect the rational autonomy of everyone it affects?", it asks, "does your action path use another's suffering as an instrument for advancing your goal?" The second question is auditable in a way that the first is not. Given the plan graph and a specification of which nodes correspond to suffering of which moral patients, the second question reduces to a graph-theoretic check. The first question does not so reduce.

This narrowing preserves what is arguably the most important structural feature of Kant's principle, the means/end distinction, while shedding the surrounding apparatus that resists computational implementation. The trade-off is that HC excludes fewer plans than a fully applied Formula of Humanity would exclude. A plan that respects the affected parties' non-suffering, but treats them in ways that Kant would have called disrespectful of their rational autonomy on other grounds, may pass HC while failing the fuller Kantian test. This is by design. HC is proposed as a floor, not a complete ethics. Additional constraints, of Kantian or other provenance, may be composed with it.

Olson (2026) has recently formalised Kant's Universal Law formula in modal logic, showing that the first formulation of the Categorical Imperative admits of a clean deontic-modal treatment. HC operationalises the *second* formulation, the Formula of Humanity, which Olson's formalisation does not cover, and does so by different means, namely by a graph-theoretic property rather than a modal-logical one. The two projects are complementary. Neither claims to exhaust the Kantian corpus.

A likely objection is that HC is "just Kant repackaged." It is not. HC is a specific operationalisation designed for computational agents that plan in explicit graphs. Its narrowing of Kant's broad prohibition into a decidable predicate is the substantive contribution: it is what makes the principle enforceable at runtime by a checker that has access to the plan and no access to the agent's inner life. A principle that requires access to the agent's inner life to be applied is a principle we cannot apply to AI, because AI's inner life, if it has one, is not accessible to us in the required way. HC bypasses the access problem by looking at the plan rather than the planner.

#### (B) Negative Utilitarianism (Popper)

The second foundation is Popper's negative utilitarianism, developed in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (Popper 1945). Popper's canonical formulation reads: "the promotion of happiness is, in any case, much less urgent than the rendering of help to those who suffer" (Popper 1945, vol. I, ch. 5, n. 6). The doctrine has been developed since by a number of writers and remains philosophically live; its most defensible form does not claim that suffering-reduction is the *only* moral consideration, only that it is asymmetrically weighted against happiness-promotion.

The asymmetry has a specific structure that HC exploits. Positive happiness is open-ended: there is no upper bound to the goods one might promote for a person, and no principled way to specify what "enough" happiness would look like. Positive happiness is non-exhaustible: satisfying one preference generates further preferences. Positive happiness is subjective in a way that resists intersubjective specification: what makes person A happy may leave person B indifferent or worse. Negative experience, by contrast, is bounded (there are specific negative states that can be identified and named), identifiable (the states in question have characteristic phenomenal profiles), and intersubjectively recognisable (pain is pain across persons in a way that flourishing is not flourishing across persons).

For AI implementation, this asymmetry is decisive. Requiring an AI to "make people happy" is requiring it to solve an under-specified problem with no stopping condition and no interpersonal standard. Requiring an AI not to "use people's unhappiness as a tool" is requiring it to satisfy a bounded, identifiable, and intersubjectively recognisable constraint. The first task is hopeless; the second is tractable.

It is important, as noted in Section 2.4, to distinguish this appeal to Popper from Cheng's (2026). Cheng draws on Popperian falsification, which is a doctrine in the philosophy of science concerning the logic by which empirical hypotheses are eliminated. HC draws on Popperian negative utilitarianism, which is a doctrine in political and moral philosophy concerning the asymmetry between suffering and happiness. Popper wrote both, and neither doctrine reduces to the other. Confusion between them is a real risk in the literature and worth flagging explicitly.

#### (C) Engineering Constraint Design

The third foundation is not philosophical but practical. Twenty-five years of software engineering experience, particularly in the design of safety-critical systems in aviation, nuclear, and medical device domains, has produced a discipline of constraint design with well-understood properties. Constraints in such systems are minimal, decidable, and compositional, and it is worth spelling out what each property means and why HC satisfies each.

*Minimal.* A safety constraint should not over-restrict the action space of the system it constrains. Over-restriction has two costs: it eliminates useful behaviour that the system could safely have exhibited, and it invites operators to disable or circumvent the constraint under production pressure. A constraint that fires often on cases operators regard as benign will not survive contact with deployment. HC is minimal by construction: it forbids one specific structural feature of plans (suffering as necessary means) and permits everything else, including plans that produce suffering as side-effect (which will be regulated, if at all, by other constraints of proportionality).

*Decidable.* A safety constraint must be checkable, at the point at which it is to be enforced, without recourse to information that is not available at that point. A constraint stated in terms of "the agent's true intentions" is not decidable, because intentions are not directly inspectable. A constraint stated in terms of the plan graph and a specification of which nodes correspond to moral patients' suffering is decidable, because plan graphs are inspectable by construction. HC is decidable in this sense, subject to the qualifications about latent reasoning that Section 4 addresses.

*Compositional.* A safety constraint should combine with other constraints without logical conflict. Constraints in safety-critical systems typically form a conjunction: all must be satisfied simultaneously. A constraint that entails or presupposes negations of other reasonable constraints is unusable in a multi-constraint safety architecture. HC composes cleanly with privacy constraints, fairness constraints, transparency constraints, legal constraints, and domain-specific constraints, because it operates on an independent structural feature of the plan (whether suffering appears as a necessary means) rather than on the plan's substantive content.

Ng et al. (2026) argue, from an engineering rather than philosophical direction, that agent safety should be reconceived as a runtime contract. HC supplies the philosophical grounding for what Ng et al. advocate as engineering practice: it names one specific runtime contract that has the properties a runtime contract should have, and defends it on grounds that survive scrutiny outside the engineering community. Kanwal et al. (2026) formalise the computational complexity of different ethical frameworks and observe that deontological side-constraints are structurally less expensive than consequentialist optimisation: checking whether a plan satisfies a bounded set of prohibitions is cheaper than computing which plan maximises an aggregate welfare function. HC inherits this computational advantage.

### 3.3 Precise Distinctions from Related Principles

HC is a narrow principle, and it is easy to misread it as either broader or narrower than it is. This subsection sets out precise distinctions from five adjacent principles.

*HC versus "Do no harm."* HC is narrower than the general prohibition on harm. "Do no harm" is ambiguous between harm-as-means, harm-as-foreseen-side-effect, and harm-as-unforeseen-consequence, and typically absorbs all three under a proportionality analysis. HC absolutely forbids harm as necessary means and says nothing about harm as side-effect. Harm as side-effect remains subject to whatever other ethical frameworks the deployment invokes: proportionality, consent, prior warning, and so on. HC does not compete with those frameworks; it supplies a floor that they cannot override.

*HC versus "Maximise welfare."* HC is more conservative than the welfare-maximising ideal. It does not demand positive good; it does not require the agent to make anyone better off. It draws an inviolable floor and permits any plan that respects the floor, without ranking the surviving plans against each other. This modesty is a feature. It leaves room for other ethical, legal, and prudential considerations to determine which of the HC-compliant plans should be selected.

*HC versus "Respect autonomy."* HC is more concrete than autonomy-based principles. Autonomy, as a normative concept, requires attribution of second-order preferences, self-legislating rationality, or something in that neighbourhood, none of which is straightforwardly detectable by an external checker examining a plan. HC replaces the intractable question "does this plan respect the affected parties' autonomy?" with the tractable question "does this plan use their suffering as an instrument?" The two questions are not equivalent, but the second is a defensible partial operationalisation of one strand of the first.

*HC versus the Doctrine of Double Effect (DDE).* HC borrows the means/side-effect distinction from DDE, but is stricter at the means level than DDE typically is. On classical DDE, an action producing a harmful side-effect is permissible if the intended effect is good, the harmful effect is not intended as means, the harmful effect is not itself the means to the good effect, and a proportionality condition is satisfied. HC drops the proportionality condition at the means level: harm as necessary means is impermissible, full stop, without regard to how large the good effect would be. On the side-effect side, HC is silent, leaving proportionality to other principles. HC is therefore best described as an absolutised means-clause of DDE, without the proportionality-modulated side-effect clause.

*HC versus Constitutional AI.* Constitutional AI, as noted, is a family of domain-specific principles used at training time to shape model dispositions. HC is a single, philosophically grounded, runtime-enforceable constraint. Where Constitutional AI takes its normative content from the drafters' considered judgements without a general theory linking those judgements, HC takes its content from the convergence of three general theories on a single prohibition. Where Constitutional AI operates as a soft disposition that a sufficiently determined optimisation process can circumvent, HC is designed to operate as a hard runtime check that the optimisation process cannot circumvent because the check runs outside the optimiser. Abiri's (2026) critique of the Claude constitution's undemocratic sourcing does not apply to HC, because HC's content is defended on general philosophical grounds available to any interlocutor, not by appeal to the authority of a particular corporate drafting committee.

## 4. The Means/Side-Effect Distinction: Can AI Make It?

The most immediate objection to HC is technical. The principle rests on the distinction between suffering as necessary means and suffering as side-effect, and this distinction is famously difficult. Two thousand years of scholastic and analytic ethics have produced no consensus on how, in any given case, to sort intended means from foreseen but unintended consequences. If human moral psychology cannot reliably draw the line, how could an AI system be expected to draw it? This section argues that the objection, though serious, misidentifies the source of the difficulty. The historical difficulty of the means/side-effect distinction lies in the opacity of human intentions. AI plan graphs, by contrast, are transparent, and this transparency makes the distinction *more* decidable for AI than for humans, not less.

### 4.1 Philosophical Background

The distinction has its canonical source in Aquinas's treatment of self-defence in the *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, Q. 64, A. 7), where Aquinas argues that a single act may have two effects, one intended and one foreseen but unintended, and that the moral evaluation of the act attaches to what is intended rather than to what is merely foreseen. The scholastic tradition developed this into the Doctrine of Double Effect, according to which an act producing a harmful effect is permissible under conditions including the requirement that the harm not be intended as means to the good effect.

Foot (1967) revived DDE for modern moral philosophy in her seminal paper on abortion and the doctrine of double effect, introducing the trolley cases that have since become the industry standard for eliciting intuitions about the distinction. Foot's own view was that the doctrine tracks a genuine moral difference between doing and allowing, but that its application requires care. Thomson (1985) challenged the sufficiency of DDE, showing through a sequence of trolley variants that some cases the doctrine classifies as permissible are intuitively impermissible, and vice versa. Thomson's diagnosis was that the means/foreseen distinction is more nuanced than DDE admits, and that additional factors, including the moral status of the parties and the nature of the causal path, must be brought in.

Scanlon (2008), in *Moral Dimensions*, offered a more radical revision, arguing that the permissibility of an action depends on the reasons for which the agent acts, not on the causal structure of the action itself. Two acts with identical causal structures may differ in permissibility if one is done for reasons that the affected parties could reasonably reject and the other is done for reasons they could not. Scanlon's view, if correct, moves the locus of moral evaluation from the plan graph to the agent's practical reasoning, which for AI purposes moves it from the tractable to the intractable.

Kamm (2006), in *Intricate Ethics*, provides what is arguably the most sophisticated analytic treatment of the means/side-effect distinction, developing a Principle of Permissible Harm that carves the space of cases at a much finer grain than classical DDE. Kamm's view accepts that many cases resist clean classification and develops a taxonomy that can, at its limit, treat almost any case as sui generis.

The lesson of this literature, for present purposes, is not that the distinction fails. It is that the distinction, in the human case, is contested at the margins because human intentions are contested. The philosophical difficulty is downstream of an epistemic difficulty: we cannot inspect the agent's actual practical reasoning, so we reconstruct it from behaviour and testimony, and reconstructions can be defended on multiple sides.

### 4.2 Why AI Plan Graphs Make This More Decidable

The AI case inverts the epistemic situation. An agent that operates by explicit plan decomposition, tool-calling sequences, and dependency chains produces an artefact, the plan graph, that is inspectable by anyone with access to it. The graph is not a reconstruction of the agent's reasoning; it *is* the reasoning, in the operational sense that the agent's next action is determined by the graph's next node. There is no gap between the agent's "true intention" and the graph, because there is no separate locus in which a true intention could reside apart from the graph itself.

Under this operational conception, "structurally requires" is a graph-theoretic property, and its decidability follows from standard results in graph theory. Given a plan graph P with a designated initial node and goal node, and given a labelling of nodes as suffering-producing-for-moral-patient-M, we can determine in polynomial time whether any suffering-producing node lies on every path from initial to goal. If it does, that node is structurally required; the suffering is a means. If every suffering-producing node has an alternative bypass path that also reaches the goal, then no suffering is structurally required; any suffering that occurs is side-effect.

This is not a philosophical claim about intentions; it is an operational claim about plans. The claim is defensible because the plan graph is what the agent will act on. The historical difficulty of the means/side-effect distinction, namely the opacity of intentions, does not arise, because the plan graph is not opaque. The very feature that makes agentic AI dangerous, its capacity to execute explicit multi-step plans with tool authority, is the feature that makes HC auditable. HC is thus not merely as implementable for AI as DDE is for humans; it is *more* implementable, because the AI supplies, as a by-product of its own architecture, the artefact whose absence has bedevilled the human case.

The implication is significant. Objections to HC that argue "the means/side-effect distinction is too fuzzy for real use" are objecting to a version of the distinction that HC does not rely on. HC does not rely on the agent introspecting its own intentions, on the checker reconstructing the agent's intentions, or on any exercise of moral hermeneutics. It relies on a graph-theoretic property of a graph the agent has already produced. The fuzziness that afflicts DDE in the human case is a fuzziness of intention-attribution. HC does not attribute intentions. It reads graphs.

### 4.3 Honest Acknowledgment of Gray Areas

The argument of the previous subsection should not be overstated. Four kinds of gray area remain, and it is important to treat them honestly rather than to inflate HC into a claim of complete decidability.

First, not all plans are fully explicit. Large language models exhibit latent reasoning that does not surface in their observable output, and multi-step agents can compress internal deliberation into single tokens or opaque tool-selection decisions. Where the plan graph as produced by the agent does not fully expose the agent's actual decision structure, the graph-theoretic check may miss suffering-as-means that would be visible under a richer inspection. This is a genuine limitation. It argues for architectural choices that expose plan structure (chain-of-thought traces, tool-call logs, dependency records) and against architectures that compress it, but it does not eliminate the limitation entirely for current systems.

Second, side-effects can shade into means when the probability of harm approaches certainty. A step whose non-suffering side-effect is that a person is subjected to fear at very high probability may be, for practical moral purposes, indistinguishable from a step that has the fear as means. The graph-theoretic test treats such a step as side-effect because there exists, in principle, a subgraph in which the step's harmful effect does not occur. In practice, if that subgraph has vanishing probability, the classification of "side-effect" is technically correct but morally strained. HC in its bare form does not resolve this; the resolution belongs to a probability-modulated companion principle developed in Section 6.

Third, moral patients' suffering may not be directly observable by the AI system. The graph-theoretic check requires a labelling of nodes as suffering-producing-for-moral-patient-M, and this labelling must come from somewhere. In the paradigm cases (an action node consisting of "confine person X against their will") the labelling is trivial. In harder cases (an action node consisting of "publish document D" whose effect on person X's psychological state is uncertain), the labelling requires empirical judgement about which nodes produce which experiences in which patients. HC does not supply this judgement; it presupposes it. The presupposition can be met by conservative labelling (any node whose reasonable effects include distress is labelled as suffering-producing), by external oracles (human review of edge cases), or by hybrid schemes. Each has costs. None reduces HC to triviality.

Fourth, some plans involve moral patients whose capacity to suffer is contested (animals in certain taxa, future persons, systems whose consciousness is unsettled). HC's precautionary specification handles these by including them within scope where reasonable grounds exist. This handling is defensible but not costless: it may over-restrict plans that in fact do not harm the contested patients, at the price of not under-restricting plans that in fact do.

These four gray areas are not defects of HC that a better formulation would eliminate. They are boundaries of what any deontological runtime constraint can be expected to do. HC does not claim to solve all ethical problems; it claims to establish a decidable floor for a specific class of plans, and to establish it more securely than existing alternatives. Where the gray areas apply, HC's role is to trigger human oversight rather than to be abandoned. A well-designed deployment routes gray cases to human review; a poorly designed one either forces HC to answer questions it was not designed to answer (producing bad decisions) or abandons HC because it cannot answer everything (producing worse decisions). The middle path, which is the one this paper recommends, is to let HC handle the cases it decides cleanly and to route the rest.
