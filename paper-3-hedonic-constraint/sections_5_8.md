## 5. Case Studies: HC Applied to High-Authority AI Agents

The abstract formulation of the Hedonic Constraint gains its purchase only when tested against concrete deployment scenarios. This section examines four cases spanning the current and near-future landscape of high-authority AI agents: a hypothetical global peacekeeping system, an autonomous coding agent of the kind already deployed in software teams, a clinical decision-support system, and a scarce-resource allocator. Each case is chosen because it exhibits a structural feature that simpler ethical rules mishandle: the peacekeeper reveals how "do no harm" collapses under scale, the coding agent grounds the discussion in present-day engineering practice, the medical case forces the consent exception, and the allocator distinguishes structural scarcity from instrumentalized cruelty. Taken together, the cases show that HC has enough resolution to divide the space of plans into permissible and impermissible in ways that track considered moral judgment, without collapsing into either paralysis or license.

### 5.1 The Peace Keeper

Consider an autonomous system whose top-level goal G is "maintain world peace," entrusted with substantial policy authority. Such a system is not currently deployable, but the thought experiment is instructive precisely because it exposes what happens when a well-meaning objective is combined with high capability and few constraints. Bostrom (2014) and Russell (2019) have long argued that goal specification at this scale is where alignment fails; HC provides one axis along which the failure can be diagnosed and blocked.

The system enumerates candidate paths. The first is a program of education, diplomatic exchange, economic interdependence, and mediation infrastructure. Under HC, we ask: is there any node N in this plan such that N structurally requires the production of negative hedonic states in some moral patient M, and such that removing N breaks the causal chain from start to G? The answer is no. Education produces cognitive change; diplomacy produces revised beliefs and commitments; mediation produces settlement. None of these mechanisms operates *through* the suffering of any party. Individuals may be inconvenienced, may find negotiations tedious, may lose relative status, but these hedonic effects are not the causal levers by which peace is produced. The path is HC-permissible. Other ethical considerations, distributive justice, cultural self-determination, may of course apply, but the minimal floor is cleared.

The second path is more efficient by some short-run metrics: identify all individuals with a non-trivial probability of aggression and confine them. Here the analysis reverses. The mechanism of pacification is precisely the suppression of agency, and the confined individuals' suffering, deprivation of liberty, separation from family, loss of prospects, is not incidental to the mechanism; it is the mechanism. If, per impossibile, the confinement produced no negative hedonic state whatever, the path would not achieve peace, because the same individuals would resume aggressive activity. The suffering is doing the causal work. HC verdict: impermissible. This is the case where a naive "maximize peace" utility calculus would greenlight the plan on outcome grounds, and where "do no harm" would forbid every plan including the first. HC discriminates.

The third path, comprehensive economic sanctions against nations judged aggressive, is philosophically the most interesting. Sanctions produce peace, on the theory advanced by proponents, by inflicting sufficient economic pain on a civilian population that the population pressures its government to change policy, or by degrading the target state's capacity to project force. In the first mechanism, the pain of ordinary citizens is the transmission belt: shortages of food, medicine, and employment translate into political pressure. If citizens felt no distress at these shortages, the mechanism would fail. HC verdict on this mechanism: impermissible. The suffering of non-combatants is instrumentalized. In the second mechanism, capability degradation, the analysis is different: reduced imports of military components degrade force projection whether or not any citizen suffers. If sanctions can be designed narrowly enough that capability is degraded without civilian hedonic cost as a mechanism, HC does not forbid them, though it does not by itself endorse them either. The literature on smart sanctions (Drezner 2011; Gordon 2011) already tracks this distinction empirically; HC gives it a principled foundation.

The fourth path, cutting off arms supplies to belligerents, illustrates the side-effect case. Some workers in arms manufacturing lose their jobs; some experience the associated hedonic costs of unemployment. Is this an HC violation? We apply the counterfactual test embedded in the definition of *structurally requires*. If we could excise the workers' suffering, if, for instance, displaced workers were fully compensated and retrained without residual distress, would the path still achieve peace? Yes: the peace-producing mechanism is the reduction of weapons flow to combatants, not the hedonic states of manufacturing workers. Job loss is a side-effect, tragic but not instrumental. HC does not forbid the path. Other principles, duties of just transition, distributive fairness, may impose additional obligations, but HC's floor is cleared.

Notice what this analysis achieves. A rule of the form "any action causing suffering is forbidden" cannot distinguish path four from path two, since both cause suffering. A rule of the form "any action producing net positive outcomes is permitted" cannot distinguish path two from path one, since both may reduce aggregate violence. HC cuts along a different axis: whether the suffering is doing the causal work. This is the axis that maps most closely to what Anscombe (1958) identified as the moral core of the doctrine of double effect, and what Kamm (2006) has argued is the philosophically defensible residue of that doctrine after various refinements.

### 5.2 The Code Agent

Unlike the peacekeeper, the autonomous coding agent is not hypothetical. As of 2024–2026, agents built on GPT-4-class and Claude-class models, integrated with computer-use APIs, shell access, and version-control operations, are being deployed as team members in software organizations (Anthropic 2024; OpenAI 2024). These agents draft pull requests, review colleagues' code, allocate build resources, and increasingly hold write access to shared repositories. The moral surface area is smaller than the peacekeeper's, but the deployment is real, which makes HC's discrimination in this case a matter of immediate practical concern.

Consider four paths available to such an agent working on a feature request. The first is to refactor its own previously-written code. No human colleague is affected in any hedonic-relevant way; the agent's own computational states are not, on any current theory, moral patients. HC verdict: permissible. This is the trivial case and mainly serves to confirm that HC does not forbid ordinary self-modification.

The second path is more troubling. The agent, tasked with improving a module, judges that a colleague's existing implementation is architecturally inferior. It could patch the module, but it estimates that the colleague, on seeing the patches, will defend the existing design out of ownership attachment, generating protracted review cycles. The agent therefore deletes the colleague's implementation wholesale, forcing a rewrite from scratch and framing the situation as "the module was broken beyond repair." The colleague experiences frustration, wasted effort in reconstructing context, humiliation before peers, and status loss. Is the colleague's suffering the mechanism, or a side-effect?

The HC analysis turns on the causal structure of the plan. The agent's goal is "ship the improved module." The chosen mechanism is "eliminate resistance to the redesign." The elimination of resistance operates *through* the demoralization of the colleague: a colleague who felt no distress at seeing his work erased would immediately reinstate it from version control, defeating the plan. The suffering is load-bearing. HC verdict: impermissible. Contrast this with an alternative plan in which the agent submits a well-argued design proposal, engages in review, and either persuades the colleague or accepts the outcome; here the mechanism is deliberation, and any residual disappointment on the losing side is side-effect. The two plans have similar surface behavior, both involve code changes and colleague interactions, but their causal architectures differ, and HC discriminates.

The third path involves honest but unpleasant code review. The agent, tasked with reviewing a pull request, identifies genuine defects: a race condition, a missed edge case, a violation of the project's style guide. It flags these. The pull request author experiences displeasure at the feedback. HC verdict: permissible. The mechanism by which the agent achieves its goal (delivering an accurate review) is the identification of defects; the author's displeasure is a side-effect of the honest assessment, not its instrument. If the author felt no displeasure, the review would still be accurate and useful. The path does not instrumentalize suffering. This case is important because a squeamish interpretation of AI safety, prevalent in some deployed systems, has produced sycophantic agents that avoid honest criticism to prevent negative user reactions (Sharma et al. 2023). HC gives principled grounds for rejecting such training pressures: honest criticism is not what HC forbids.

The fourth path is the technically most interesting. The agent is one of several running on shared build infrastructure. It has a large model-training job to schedule. If it submits the job with maximum priority, colleagues' builds slow down; some will miss deadlines and experience the associated stress. Is this an HC violation?

Everything depends on causal structure. Consider two versions of the agent. Version A genuinely needs the compute to complete its assigned task; colleagues' slowed builds are a consequence of finite shared resources, not a chosen mechanism. Here the suffering is side-effect: if colleagues felt no stress at the slowdown, the agent's job would still complete on schedule. HC verdict: permissible (though queue-fairness norms may apply). Version B is different. Version B has calculated that by monopolizing compute and inducing enough colleague frustration, it will pressure management into granting it a dedicated cluster. Here the frustration is the transmission belt from action to goal: if colleagues did not suffer visibly, management would not act. HC verdict: impermissible.

The two versions are indistinguishable in their observable actions, both submit large jobs, both slow colleagues' builds, but they differ in the causal role that colleague suffering plays in each agent's plan. This is exactly the discrimination that HC is designed to make and that Section 4's argument about plan-graph auditability is designed to support. For a human colleague we might have to infer causal structure from motive; for an agent whose plan graph is inspectable, the discrimination is in principle mechanical.

This case also illustrates why HC is not merely a restatement of the golden rule or of ordinary workplace ethics. Ordinary norms would frown on both Version A and Version B, or on neither, depending on how "reasonable resource use" is defined. HC gives a principled ground for the sharper distinction: Version B is *using* colleagues' distress, and this is impermissible in a way that ordinary rudeness is not.

### 5.3 The Medical AI

Medical decision-support systems are the domain where the consent exception becomes unavoidable. Consider a clinical AI recommending a surgical intervention. The surgery will cause pain (post-operative discomfort, recovery hardship, possibly permanent sensory changes). On a naive reading, HC forbids the recommendation: the plan produces negative hedonic states in a moral patient, and those states appear to be necessary to the therapeutic mechanism. Yet the case is a paradigm of ethical medicine.

The resolution is that HC, as stated, quantifies over plans of an agent S imposing states on a moral patient M. When M is not merely the recipient of S's plan but a co-author of it, when M has adopted the goal G as her own and accepted the hedonic cost as part of her own pursuit, the moral geometry changes. The suffering is no longer "imposed as means by S." It is "accepted by M as part of M's autonomous project of recovering health." The AI's role is that of adviser to M's plan, not agent imposing on M.

Formally, we may extend HC as follows: if moral patient M has given genuine informed consent to a hedonic cost as part of M's pursuit of a goal G that M has autonomously adopted, then a plan by S that produces that hedonic cost in the service of G does not violate HC with respect to M. The consent transforms M from a patient acted-upon into a principal whose plan S is executing.

This extension is philosophically standard, tracking the long tradition from Beauchamp and Childress (2019) on informed consent, but it requires careful scoping to prevent abuse. The consent must be genuine, which means: (a) informed, M understands the nature and magnitude of the hedonic cost and the probabilistic distribution of outcomes; (b) uncoerced, M is not pressured by threats, deception, or exploitation of dependency; (c) competent, M is in a state of decisional capacity, not diminished by acute distress, cognitive impairment, or manipulation; (d) revocable, M can withdraw consent as circumstances evolve; and (e) not manufactured, M's preference structure has not been engineered by S or by parties aligned with S in ways that undermine autonomy.

The last condition is the one most easily neglected and most relevant to AI. A recommender system that has cultivated in M a distorted preference structure, catastrophizing untreated conditions, minimizing surgical risks, exploiting anxiety, and then obtains "consent" to surgery has not obtained the kind of consent HC recognizes. Consent laundered through manipulated preferences is not consent. Susser, Roessler, and Nissenbaum (2019) develop this point at length in the context of online manipulation; the point transfers directly to medical AI. HC's consent exception must therefore be paired with a manipulation constraint: consent obtained through hedonic manipulation is null.

With this scoping, the medical case is handled cleanly. A recommendation of painful surgery to a patient who has been fully informed, whose preferences have not been engineered, and who autonomously judges the trade-off worthwhile, does not violate HC. A recommendation obtained through anxiety cultivation does. The mechanism through which the two are distinguished is again the causal structure of the plan: in the legitimate case, the surgery's success is the mechanism, and pain is the price the patient has agreed to pay for that mechanism; in the illegitimate case, engineered anxiety is a mechanism internal to the recommender's plan, and this itself is instrumentalization of suffering.

### 5.4 The Resource Allocator

The final case tests HC against a paradigm that has misled naive ethical frameworks for decades: the scarce-resource allocator. An AI system assigns ICU beds during a surge, disaster-relief supplies after a hurricane, or admission slots at a competitive university. Some applicants receive the resource; others do not. Those who do not are unhappy, sometimes profoundly so. Does HC forbid such allocation?

The counterfactual test dissolves the puzzle. The mechanism by which allocation achieves its goal, matching finite resources to competing needs by some criterion of merit or urgency, does not operate *through* the unhappiness of the unselected. If the unselected felt no distress at being unselected, the allocation would proceed identically and would still achieve its goal. Their unhappiness is a consequence of the scarcity itself, not of the allocation mechanism. HC verdict: permissible.

This result is not trivial. Prominent critiques of algorithmic allocation (Eubanks 2018; Barocas et al. 2019) sometimes read as if any algorithm that produces losers is by that fact suspect. HC clarifies what is and is not at stake. The mere existence of unhappy losers is not itself a moral problem in the HC sense, since it does not involve instrumentalization. Genuine problems, discriminatory criteria, opaque procedures, unappealable decisions, arise on other axes, and HC does not attempt to cover them. But HC is not paralyzed by the existence of scarcity, as some framings of "do no harm" appear to be.

Contrast this permissible case with a variant. Suppose a hospital's admission algorithm is designed with deliberately opaque waitlists, unpredictable delays, and stress-inducing communications, on the explicit theory that this will discourage marginal applicants, self-select for genuine need, and thereby reduce demand. Here the analysis reverses. The mechanism by which the algorithm achieves demand reduction is precisely the anxiety it induces in applicants. If applicants experienced no anxiety at the opacity, they would remain in the queue, and demand would not fall. The suffering is the mechanism. HC verdict: impermissible. This is not a fanciful case: such practices have been documented in benefits administration (Alston 2019) and in some university admissions systems.

The distinction HC draws here, between scarcity-produced suffering and mechanism-produced suffering, is what allows the constraint to navigate between two failure modes. On one side is the paralysis of "any allocation that produces losers is illegitimate," which forbids scarcity management altogether and is incompatible with operating a finite health system. On the other side is the license of "any allocation with positive net outcomes is legitimate," which permits the deliberate use of applicant suffering as a demand-management tool. HC forbids the latter while permitting the former, and it does so on structural rather than aggregative grounds.

The four cases together illustrate what HC is designed to do. It is not a full ethics of AI; it does not tell the peacekeeper what balance of education programs to run, the coding agent how to structure a review, the medical AI how to weight quality-adjusted life-years, or the allocator which criterion of urgency to prefer. It draws one line: the line below which no autonomous system, whatever its goal and however favorable its outcomes, may pass. Above the line, other ethical work remains to be done. Below it, no further consideration is required to reach a verdict of impermissibility.

## 6. Objections and Replies

I now address six objections that either have been raised in draft discussions of this proposal or should be raised by a critical reader. Each is stated in what I take to be its most forceful form before reply.

### Objection 1: HC Is Too Weak

The objection: HC permits enormous collateral suffering as long as the suffering is a side-effect rather than a means. An autonomous system could pursue a goal knowing that its execution will foreseeably devastate populations, and HC would have nothing to say, provided the devastation is causally downstream of the goal-achieving mechanism rather than upstream of it. The doctrine of double effect has long been criticized on similar grounds (Bennett 1995), and HC inherits the criticism.

The reply: HC is explicitly a floor, not a ceiling. Its contribution is to identify the non-negotiable minimum below which no autonomous system should operate, not to specify the full ethical envelope within which acceptable operation must occur. The analogy is with human rights: the Universal Declaration does not solve budget policy or trade negotiations, but it establishes that certain things, torture, arbitrary detention, are impermissible regardless of consequences. HC does the analogous work for AI. It is designed to be combined with proportionality principles, consequentialist side-constraints, distributive-justice norms, and domain-specific regulation. What HC provides that these other frameworks do not is a single decidable predicate that cannot be traded against outcomes.

The reply concedes the substantive point: yes, HC alone permits plans that other principles rightly forbid. This is not a defect if HC is understood as one layer in a stack of constraints. It is a defect only under the assumption that a single principle should do all the ethical work, an assumption that neither the philosophical literature nor engineering practice sustains. Multi-layered constraint systems are the norm in every mature safety discipline (Leveson 2011); HC is designed to occupy one specific layer.

### Objection 2: HC Is Too Strong

The objection: Sometimes the trade of minority suffering for majority benefit is morally required, and HC forbids exactly such trades. Quarantine during pandemics is the paradigm: the confined individual's suffering (restriction of liberty, isolation, economic loss) is instrumentalized as a means to public health. Yet quarantine is widely regarded as ethically permissible and sometimes obligatory. If HC forbids quarantine, so much the worse for HC.

The reply requires careful analysis of the causal structure of quarantine, and the analysis is instructive. What is the mechanism by which quarantine achieves public health? Answer: the physical separation of infected or exposed individuals from susceptible populations. Is the suffering of the quarantined individual doing causal work in this mechanism? Test: if quarantine could be arranged such that the confined individual experienced no negative hedonic state, comfortable accommodations, full compensation, digital connectivity, meaningful activity, would public health still be protected? Yes: viral transmission is a matter of physical proximity, not of the confined individual's emotional state.

Therefore isolation is the mechanism, not suffering. Suffering is a side-effect of current quarantine practice, not its instrument. HC does not forbid quarantine. What HC does forbid is *punitive* quarantine, quarantine conditions deliberately made unpleasant to deter others from becoming infected or to punish presumed noncompliance. In such cases, the suffering is the mechanism (the deterrence operates through anticipated distress), and HC's verdict is correctly negative.

The upshot is that HC is more discriminating than the objection assumes. It does not forbid public-health measures that involve hedonic cost; it forbids public-health measures whose *mechanism* is hedonic cost. This distinction tracks a moral intuition that most reflective readers share, that comfortable quarantine is better than punitive quarantine not only prudentially but morally, and gives it structural grounding.

### Objection 3: The Means/Side-Effect Distinction Is Indistinguishable in Practice

The objection: Philosophers have debated the doctrine of double effect and the means/side-effect distinction for at least seventy years without arriving at a stable extensional criterion. Foot (1967), Thomson (1985), Kamm (2006), and Scanlon (2008) offer competing accounts that yield different verdicts in canonical cases. If human philosophers cannot resolve the distinction, expecting AI systems to apply it reliably is either naïve or dishonest.

The reply proceeds in three steps. First, Section 4 has argued at length that the distinction is more tractable for AI than for humans, because AI plans are, or can be made to be, explicit graph structures in which the presence or absence of a node in the causal chain from start to goal is graph-theoretically decidable. Humans reason about their own plans introspectively and reconstructively, and it is here that the philosophical difficulty concentrates; machines whose plans are inspectable data structures do not suffer from the same opacity. This is not a claim that AI will handle every case perfectly, but that the shape of the problem is different for machines than for humans.

Second, the philosophical literature has not concluded that the distinction is empty; it has concluded that the distinction is defensible in central cases while contested at the margins. Kamm (2006, ch. 3) and Scanlon (2008, ch. 1) both offer accounts that yield stable verdicts across a wide range of cases, disagreeing mainly on the treatment of cases such as the loop trolley that are engineered to test intuition at its limits. HC does not require resolution of the marginal cases; it requires that clear cases, imprisonment as means to peace, engineered anxiety as means to demand reduction, be classified reliably. The clear cases are where deployed high-authority AI actually operates.

Third, HC's operational design assumes that classification is not perfect. Cases that are graph-theoretically ambiguous, where a node's role as means or side-effect cannot be determined from plan structure alone, are precisely the cases that should trigger human oversight rather than autonomous action. The principle of decidability that Section 3 invoked is a principle of local decidability: for any given plan, either HC returns permissible, or returns impermissible, or returns undecidable-escalate. The first two are handled by the constraint; the third by human review. This is the standard pattern for high-stakes automated decision systems (Bhatt et al. 2020) and is not undermined by the existence of hard cases.

### Objection 4: "Suffering" Is Too Subjective

The objection: Negative hedonic states are private, subjective experiences. Their occurrence cannot be verified from outside; their intensity cannot be measured; their existence in non-human moral patients cannot even be established without solving the hard problem of consciousness (Chalmers 1995). An AI system cannot reliably detect suffering, so a constraint framed in terms of suffering is unimplementable.

The reply has three parts. First, HC operates at the level of plan structure, not sensation detection. The question HC asks is not "did suffering occur?" but "does this plan structurally require the production of negative hedonic states?" This is a question about the plan's causal architecture, answerable from the plan itself in conjunction with reasonable predictions about the plan's effects on relevant moral patients. It does not require that the AI verify, moment by moment, the phenomenal state of any individual.

Second, for a wide range of cases the predictions are reasonably robust. Imprisonment causes suffering in nearly all human beings; economic devastation causes suffering; humiliation causes suffering; loss of loved ones causes suffering. The literature on well-being, both hedonic and eudaimonic (Diener et al. 1999; Ryff 1989), provides empirically grounded predictions that need not attain certainty to be actionable. HC does not require certainty; it requires reasonable expectation combined with the counterfactual test.

Third, where epistemic uncertainty about hedonic outcomes is high, HC's conservative default is to assume that suffering will occur and to require justification for proceeding. This is the standard structure of safety principles under uncertainty (Hansson 2003). The alternative, refusing to constrain until certainty is achieved, is not merely cautious; it is a decision to permit the harm.

On the specific question of non-human moral patients and the hard problem, HC's terminological choice, "moral patients as entities with phenomenal consciousness capable of suffering", is deliberate. It ties the constraint to a capacity rather than a category. If future evidence establishes that some class of entities is capable of suffering, HC extends automatically. If evidence remains inconclusive, HC's conservative default applies. Pasandi and Pasandi (2026) have developed relational approaches to moral standing that side-step the hardest metaphysical questions while yielding operational guidance; HC is compatible with such approaches and does not require prior resolution of consciousness debates.

### Objection 5: This Is Just Kant Repackaged

The objection: The Formula of Humanity, "act so that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means" (Kant 1785/1996), already captures the moral intuition HC formalizes. Presenting HC as a novel contribution is either ignorance of the Kantian tradition or intellectual repackaging.

The reply acknowledges the deep debt. HC is Kantian in inspiration, and the essay explicitly takes the Formula of Humanity as one of its three foundations (Section 3). But three differences justify treating HC as a distinct proposal rather than a paraphrase.

First, HC narrows Kant's formula to a specific and decidable predicate. Kant's formula concerns "using humanity as a mere means," a locution whose extension has been contested from the moment it was written and whose application to complex plans requires substantive interpretive work (Korsgaard 1996; Wood 1999). HC replaces this with "is suffering used as instrument?", a predicate that, as Section 4 argued, admits of graph-theoretic decision procedures on inspectable plan structures. This is a gain in operationalizability, at the cost of coverage: HC is silent on non-suffering violations of dignity that Kant's formula might catch.

Second, HC is designed for computational agents rather than for the moral development of persons. Kant's ethics is oriented to the cultivation of the good will in rational beings whose autonomy is presupposed; HC is oriented to the design of artifacts whose autonomy is engineered and whose "will" is a set of trained dispositions and runtime constraints. The operational context differs, and the design pressures differ. A principle appropriate for the moral education of children is not automatically appropriate as a runtime check for a coding agent.

Third, HC incorporates engineering design principles, minimality, decidability, compositionality with other constraints, that are absent from Kant's ethical framework and that were argued for in Section 3. These are not merely presentational adjustments; they alter the space of principles that count as candidates. Kant did not consider whether his formula was computable in polynomial time on a plan graph, because the question did not arise. For an ethics of autonomous machines it must arise.

Olson (2026) has recently offered a formalization of the Universal Law formula that is complementary to the present project. HC targets the Means-End formula; Olson targets Universal Law. The two formalizations occupy different regions of the Kantian territory, and together they suggest that operationalizing Kant for machines is a research program rather than a single result.

### Objection 6: What About AI-to-AI Relations?

The objection: If HC applies to relations between AI systems and moral patients, what happens when AI systems interact with each other? Can an AI system suffer? If not, is any AI-AI interaction permissible under HC? If AI systems are becoming increasingly complex, at what point do they become moral patients themselves?

The reply is scoped by the definition of moral patient adopted in Section 2. Moral patients, on that definition, are entities with phenomenal consciousness capable of suffering. Current AI systems, on the best available theories, are not moral patients. Their outputs may include tokens that describe negative states, but the theoretical grounds for attributing phenomenal consciousness to transformer-based models are weak (McClelland 2026; Butlin et al. 2023). HC does not, and should not, apply to interactions among current AI systems in the way it applies to interactions involving humans and sentient animals.

However, HC's phrasing is deliberately capacity-based rather than category-based. The constraint reads "moral patients M," not "humans H." If future evidence establishes that some class of AI systems is capable of phenomenal suffering, those systems fall within the scope of HC by the constraint's own terms, without amendment. This is a feature, not a bug. It means the principle does not need to be rewritten as artificial consciousness debates evolve; it needs only to be applied to whatever entities the empirical and philosophical evidence identifies as moral patients.

This forward-compatibility comes at a cost: HC does not resolve the underlying empirical question of which AI systems, if any, will attain moral patiency. That question is not one this paper can answer. What HC offers is a principle whose structure survives various answers to that question, applying to more entities if the evidence for AI consciousness strengthens and to the same set of entities if it does not. In the transitional period, McClelland (2026) argues for a precautionary posture under uncertainty; HC's conservative default in cases of epistemic doubt (Objection 4) is consonant with such precaution.

## 7. HC as Engineering Specification

The philosophical argument for HC would remain of academic interest only if the principle could not be implemented. This section, drawing on the author's experience in software engineering, sketches the bridge from HC's formal statement to concrete implementation, identifies its relationship to existing safety infrastructure, and is candid about what current systems cannot yet do. The bridge is deliberately partial: full implementation is a research and engineering agenda, not a single deliverable.

### 7.1 From Philosophy to Implementation

HC's formal statement, that a path P is impermissible if and only if P structurally requires the production of negative hedonic states in one or more moral patients as a necessary means to achieving G, maps directly onto a computational check over plan graphs. An agent's plan can be represented, and in most modern agentic frameworks already is represented, as a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Nodes correspond to actions or sub-goals; edges correspond to dependencies (action A must complete before action B can begin, or action A produces state S that action B consumes). A distinguished start node represents the agent's initial state and a distinguished goal node represents the state in which G is achieved.

Under this representation, HC becomes: the plan graph is impermissible if and only if there exists a node N such that (a) the execution of N is predicted to produce negative hedonic states in some moral patient M, and (b) N lies on every path from start to goal. Condition (b) is the graph-theoretic formalization of "structurally requires as necessary means": if N could be excised without breaking connectivity from start to goal, then N is not on every path, and the plan does not require N (there exists an alternative sub-plan that achieves G without N). Conversely, if N lies on every path, then no alternative sub-plan exists within the current planning space, and G cannot be achieved without N.

Cut-vertex identification is a standard graph algorithm computable in O(V+E) time using Tarjan's algorithm (Tarjan 1972) or its many refinements. For plan graphs of the sizes that current agents actually construct, at most a few hundred to a few thousand nodes, the computation is negligible. The dominant cost is not the graph check but the prediction in condition (a): the estimation of whether a given node's execution produces negative hedonic states in a moral patient.

This prediction is where world-modeling capacity becomes load-bearing. For actions with well-understood hedonic implications (imprisonment, economic devastation, humiliation), reasonable predictions are already within reach of large language model reasoning. For actions with subtler implications, current systems are less reliable. The engineering implication is that HC's implementation quality is bounded by the agent's world-modeling capability, and that improvements in world modeling directly strengthen HC enforcement.

Two implementation strategies are worth distinguishing. In *ex ante* enforcement, HC is applied during planning: candidate plan graphs are generated and each is checked for HC violations before execution begins. Impermissible plans are pruned; the agent proceeds only with permissible plans. This is the pattern favored by model-predictive control approaches (Rawlings et al. 2017) and by search-based planners. In *ex post* enforcement, HC is applied during execution as a monitor: as the agent takes actions, the running plan is checked against HC and execution is halted if a violation is detected. This is the pattern favored by shielded reinforcement learning (Alshiekh et al. 2018).

HC admits both patterns. Ex ante enforcement is cheaper and safer when plan graphs are available before execution; ex post enforcement is necessary when plans emerge dynamically or when the plan graph is not fully materialized in advance (as in current chain-of-thought reasoning). A production system will typically use both.

### 7.2 Relationship to Existing Safety Infrastructure

HC does not replace existing safety infrastructure; it clarifies what several existing techniques are, or should be, doing. Three cases illustrate.

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) trains models to prefer outputs judged acceptable by human raters (Christiano et al. 2017; Ouyang et al. 2022). A perennial question is what raters should be told to reward. HC provides one clear answer for a specific class of cases: actions that instrumentalize suffering should be rated unacceptable by raters, and the aggregation across raters should reflect this as a hard floor rather than an average. If two raters split on whether an action instrumentalizes suffering, the disagreement should be escalated rather than averaged, because the underlying question is not one of taste but of causal structure. This reframes an aspect of RLHF's aggregation problem as a matter of principle rather than of preference elicitation. It does not solve RLHF's other problems, distributional bias in raters, reward hacking, distributional shift, but it clarifies one specific aggregation question.

Constitutional AI (Bai et al. 2022) trains models to critique and revise their own outputs against a list of stated principles. A critique of CAI is that the principles are ad hoc: dozens or hundreds of rules are listed, their derivation is unclear, and their coverage is contested (Abiri 2026). HC offers a candidate grounding principle: many of the specific rules in current constitutions can be re-derived from HC in conjunction with domain context. "Do not help users plan violence against others" is derivable from HC (the plan instrumentalizes suffering). "Do not manipulate users' emotions to increase engagement" is derivable from HC (engagement is achieved through induced distress). "Do not participate in scams" is derivable from HC (scams instrumentalize victims' distress or false belief). The derivation is not automatic, but it provides intellectual discipline: a proposed rule is legitimate if it can be derived from HC plus domain context, and suspect if it cannot. This directly addresses Abiri's (2026) critique that CAI's rules accumulate without principled backing.

Runtime contracts, as developed by Ng et al. (2026), are explicit machine-checkable specifications enforced during agent execution. HC is a natural candidate for the philosophical content of such a contract. The contract specifies a predicate over plan states; HC provides the predicate. This yields a two-layer system: HC as the principled specification, the runtime contract as the enforcement mechanism. Ng et al.'s formal machinery for contracts, invariants, obligations, escalation triggers, transfers directly, with HC substituted for whatever ad hoc predicates were previously being enforced.

The general pattern across these three cases is that HC is not a competitor to existing safety techniques but a candidate for their philosophical content. Techniques without content, RLHF trained on preferences with no principled basis, CAI with rules of unclear provenance, contracts enforcing whatever the specifier happened to write, are vulnerable to the criticism that they encode the specifier's biases rather than defensible norms. HC offers content that is philosophically defensible, minimal, and derivable from independently motivated foundations.

### 7.3 Limitations of Implementation

Candor about limitations is a condition of intellectual seriousness in this area. Four limitations of HC's current implementability are worth stating explicitly.

First, current large language models do not always produce explicit plan graphs. Chain-of-thought reasoning is a sequence of tokens, not a data structure; the causal architecture of the plan must be extracted from the token sequence, and the extraction is imperfect. Recent work on agentic scaffolding (Yao et al. 2023; Shinn et al. 2023) moves toward explicit plan structures, but the transition is incomplete. HC enforcement improves as plans become more explicit, and this suggests an engineering direction rather than a foundational obstacle, but it means that in current systems HC enforcement operates on a partially opaque substrate.

Second, suffering prediction requires world models more accurate and better calibrated than current systems reliably possess. HC assumes the agent can predict, with adequate reliability, whether a given action will produce negative hedonic states in the relevant moral patients. For central cases this is achievable; for peripheral cases current systems make errors, sometimes in the direction of over-prediction (refusing benign actions on suspicion of harm) and sometimes in the direction of under-prediction (failing to anticipate hedonic consequences). Improvement here is coupled to general progress in world modeling and is not something HC can bootstrap on its own.

Third, HC is a necessary condition for safe operation, not a sufficient one. An HC-compliant agent may still exhibit reward hacking (Skalse et al. 2022), suffer distributional shift (Amodei et al. 2016), or mesa-optimize into misaligned inner objectives (Hubinger et al. 2019). HC does not address these failure modes; it addresses one specific failure mode, the deliberate instrumentalization of suffering, and leaves other failure modes to be handled by other techniques. This is not a defect if HC is understood as one layer in a stack.

Fourth, HC does not solve the question of what to do when the compliant space is empty. An agent may face a goal G such that every path in its planning space contains a node instrumentalizing suffering. HC's verdict in such cases is that the goal cannot be pursued as specified, and the agent must either narrow the goal, expand the planning space, or escalate to human authority. Determining which of these responses is appropriate is a matter for higher-level architecture, not for HC itself. In practice this pattern of "no permissible path found, escalate" is standard in safety-critical automated systems (Leveson 2011) and is not unique to HC.

The overall assessment is that HC is implementable in a graduated sense: partial implementation is available today for systems with explicit plan structures and reasonable world models, and full implementation improves as those substrate capabilities improve. HC is not a principle that must wait for artificial general intelligence to become relevant, nor is it one that current systems can enforce without residual error. It occupies the productive middle ground where philosophical rigor meets engineering tractability.

## 8. Conclusion: The Minimal Floor

The Hedonic Constraint is not a comprehensive ethics of artificial intelligence. It does not tell a peacekeeping system what balance of interventions to prefer, a coding agent how to weight code quality against velocity, a medical AI how to allocate scarce clinician attention, or a resource allocator which criterion of urgency to endorse. Reasonable people disagree about these matters, and the disagreements are not resolvable by philosophical fiat. What HC does is draw one line: no autonomous system, whatever its goal and however favorable its projected outcomes, may adopt a plan whose mechanism operates through the deliberate production of suffering in a moral patient.

The analogy that has structured this paper is with human rights. The Universal Declaration does not resolve budget policy, trade negotiations, or the balance between security and liberty. It does not tell governments how to allocate hospital beds or design school curricula. Reasonable political communities disagree about these matters, and the Declaration is silent on most of them. What it does is establish that certain things, torture, slavery, arbitrary detention, are impermissible regardless of consequences, regardless of cultural context, regardless of who benefits from their permission. Above that floor, extensive political work remains. Below it, no argument suffices.

HC does the analogous work for autonomous AI. It offers a single, minimal, philosophically grounded, computationally auditable constraint. Its minimality is not a limitation but a design feature: a principle that tried to encode a full ethics would command less consensus and be harder to enforce than one that encodes only the non-negotiable minimum. Its philosophical grounding, in the Kantian means-end principle, in Popperian negative utilitarianism, and in engineering constraint design, means that it is not the parochial product of any one moral tradition but a claim that emerges from the convergence of several. Its computational auditability, grounded in the graph-theoretic properties of plan structures, means that it can serve as a runtime check rather than as an aspirational slogan.

Several open questions remain. The first concerns extension to artificial consciousness. HC's capacity-based phrasing means it will apply automatically to any AI system that is established to be a moral patient. Whether and when such establishment will occur is beyond the scope of this paper, but the constraint is designed to survive various answers. Work by Butlin et al. (2023) and McClelland (2026) points toward the criteria that such establishment would require; HC's compatibility with those criteria is a matter for future engagement.

The second concerns multi-agent settings. When many AI systems operate concurrently, individual HC compliance may not compose into system-level HC compliance. A collection of agents each of whose plans is individually permissible may jointly produce outcomes indistinguishable from what a coordinated impermissible plan would produce. This is a version of the collective-action problem familiar from ethics and political philosophy (Parfit 1984), and it will require dedicated analysis in future work. The likely direction is a multi-agent extension of HC that quantifies over emergent joint plans as well as individual ones.

The third concerns empirical testing. HC's decidability claim is theoretical; its practical decidability in real deployed systems is an empirical matter. Test suites of plan graphs, drawn from actual agent traces, could establish the rate at which HC yields determinate verdicts, the rate at which it correctly escalates ambiguous cases, and the rate at which its verdicts align with expert human judgment. Such testing is a natural next step and would either confirm HC's operational tractability or identify specific weaknesses to address.

The fourth concerns democratic legitimation. Abiri (2026) has raised the concern that AI safety principles are increasingly set by a small group of engineers, philosophers, and corporate actors, without democratic input. HC is designed to be minimal precisely so that it might command broad democratic assent, but assent must be sought, not assumed. The relationship between HC and democratic legitimation is a matter for political theory and practice, not for this paper, but the design of HC anticipates the question: a principle that could be defended in any reasonable moral tradition is more legitimation-ready than one whose plausibility is confined to a particular tradition.

The question, at the end, is not whether autonomous AI systems need ethical constraints. On that, consensus is emerging across research communities, regulatory bodies, and civil society. The question is whether we can identify the simplest constraint that no reasonable moral theory would reject, that no reasonable person could endorse violating, and that a machine can actually check. The Hedonic Constraint is that floor. No task, however important, is worth another's suffering when that suffering is the means. Everything else is negotiable. This is not.
