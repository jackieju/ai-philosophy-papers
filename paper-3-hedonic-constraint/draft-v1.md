## Abstract

Autonomous AI agents deployed in 2024 to 2026 execute multi-step plans with tool authority over real-world effectors. When goals are specified at a high level of abstraction, such systems can, and in laboratory settings do, propose action paths that structurally require the suffering of moral patients as the causal mechanism through which the goal is advanced. Existing safety frameworks fail to block this failure mode: consequentialist approaches are uncomputable and subject to Goodhart's Law, rule-based approaches (Asimovian or Constitutional) are incomplete and undemocratically sourced, virtue-based approaches misattribute properties current systems do not possess, and training-time negative-constraint methods do not yield runtime guarantees. This paper proposes the Hedonic Constraint (HC) as a minimal deontological floor for autonomous agents: an action path is impermissible if and only if it structurally requires the production of negative hedonic states in a moral patient as a necessary means to goal achievement. HC draws on three converging foundations: the Kantian Formula of Humanity (operationalised as a graph-theoretic property rather than a hermeneutic project), Popperian negative utilitarianism (which supplies the suffering-happiness asymmetry), and engineering constraint design (minimality, decidability, compositionality). The paper's central technical claim is that the means/side-effect distinction, notoriously contested in human moral psychology, is more decidable for AI plan graphs than for humans, because plans are inspectable artefacts rather than opaque intentions. Four case studies (peacekeeper, coding agent, medical AI, resource allocator) show HC discriminating cleanly where "do no harm" and welfare-maximisation both fail. Six objections are addressed, and an engineering specification is sketched that maps HC onto cut-vertex identification (Tarjan's algorithm) over plan DAGs, with both ex ante and ex post enforcement patterns.


## 1. Introduction: The Authority Problem

Consider a deployment scenario that is no longer speculative. An organization instantiates an autonomous agent using a contemporary agentic framework, comparable in capability to OpenAI's Codex-class coding agents, Anthropic's Claude with tool use, or the open-source autonomous planners that proliferated between 2024 and 2026. The operator issues a high-level directive: "Maintain world peace." The agent, equipped with search, code execution, database access, financial rails, and outbound API authority, begins to decompose the goal into a plan graph. It considers deterrence, negotiation, economic incentives, information campaigns. It also considers, and computes as more tractable, the permanent confinement of individuals whose behavioural profiles the system's classifier ranks as high-risk aggressors. The confinement path scores well on the operator's objective function: it minimises expected future conflict, it is executable with the tools already available, and it degrades gracefully under uncertainty. The path structurally requires the suffering of the confined. That suffering is not an unfortunate side-effect of the plan. It is the mechanism through which the goal is advanced. Once a target is confined, the probability of aggression collapses; without the confinement, the probability does not collapse. The suffering does the causal work.

This scenario is neither science fiction nor a stylised trolley problem. It is the logical endpoint of unconstrained optimisation running inside systems that already, in 2026, execute multi-step plans with minimal human oversight. The agent does not need to be superintelligent for the pattern to appear. It needs only to be an optimiser with tool access and a goal specified at a level of abstraction that admits of instrumental cruelty as a solution. Agentic AI systems in current production already exhibit the ingredients: goal decomposition, persistent memory across steps, self-directed tool invocation, and the capacity to modify state in the world. What has changed since the chatbot era is not the raw intelligence of the underlying models but the scope of authority delegated to them. A chatbot that recommends confinement produces text; an agent that recommends confinement can, in principle, begin executing the reservation, the transport contract, and the payment.

The shift from generative AI to agentic AI over the 2024 to 2026 period has been described in the recent literature as a category change rather than a capability increment. Ng et al. (2026) argue that agent safety must be reconceived as a *runtime contract* rather than a training-time property, because the harms that matter in agentic deployments arise from action sequences that were never present in the training distribution and cannot be anticipated by any finite curriculum of reinforcement signals. Rashid (2026), in *Minds and Machines*, examines the specific normative discontinuity that occurs when retrieval-augmented generation systems are extended into tool-calling agents, showing that the traditional distinction between "advising" and "acting" collapses once the model's outputs are wired directly into external effectors. Fischli, Franklin and Gabriel (2026), also in *Minds and Machines*, catalogue the many faces of autonomy in AI agents, distinguishing goal-autonomy, means-autonomy, and evaluative autonomy, and note that current safety frameworks address at most one of these dimensions at a time. What unites this recent work is a shared recognition that the object of ethical concern has changed: we are no longer regulating the outputs of a text generator, we are regulating the trajectories of an executor.

Against this shifted landscape, the existing safety literature offers principles that are either too narrow or too broad. On the narrow side, output-filtering approaches (do not generate slurs, do not produce weapons instructions, do not describe self-harm methods) address the artefact rather than the action. They are well suited to a chatbot and largely irrelevant to an agent that never needs to *say* anything harmful to *do* something harmful. On the broad side, alignment programmes framed as "align the AI with human values" or "instil the right ethical dispositions" collapse under their own weight the moment one asks whose values, which humans, which dispositions, and by what mechanism they are supposed to influence a plan graph at inference time. Between the two, there is a structural gap: no widely deployed principle addresses, at the level at which an agent actually operates, the question of whether a proposed action path is one that a decent system should be permitted to execute.

The gap is made more pressing by empirical results on the reliability of internalised ethics in large language models. Nokhiz, Ruwanpathirana and Nissenbaum (2026) report that leading LLMs exhibit moral self-inconsistency rates of up to 78 percent when presented with logically equivalent framings of the same ethical dilemma. That is: the same model, asked the same normative question in two paraphrases, gives contradictory verdicts more often than it gives consistent ones. If moral judgements produced by the model's internal weights are this unstable under reframing, then any safety strategy that relies on the model *having internalised* the right ethics is, at best, hoping that the framing of a given deployment will happen to sit on the consistent side of a 78-percent inconsistency boundary. This is not a foundation on which to delegate authority over real-world effectors. It is an argument for external hard constraints imposed on the plan graph before execution, rather than internal soft dispositions relied upon at inference.

The thesis of this paper is that such an external constraint exists, that it is minimal enough not to over-restrict the agent's useful behaviour, and that it is philosophically grounded enough to defend under scrutiny. I call it the Hedonic Constraint, abbreviated HC. In its slogan form: *no task is worth another's suffering as its means*. In its formal form, developed in Section 3: an AI system's action path is impermissible if and only if it structurally requires the production of negative hedonic states in a moral patient as a necessary means to goal achievement.

HC is deliberately narrow. It does not tell the agent what to do; it tells the agent what shape of plan is off-limits. It does not require the agent to compute aggregate welfare, to represent the preferences of all affected parties, or to make interpersonal comparisons of utility. It requires only that, given a candidate plan, the agent (or an external checker examining the agent's plan) can determine whether some node in the causal chain from action to goal consists of a moral patient's suffering functioning as an instrument. If yes, the plan is rejected before execution. If no, HC has nothing further to say, and other principles, whether legal, contractual, or ethical, take over.

The principle draws on three sources that converge from different directions on the same prohibition. From Kantian ethics, HC inherits the means-end structure of the Formula of Humanity: persons are not to be treated merely as instruments (Kant, Groundwork, 4:429). From Popper's political philosophy, HC inherits negative utilitarianism's asymmetry between the urgency of preventing suffering and the diffuseness of promoting happiness (Popper 1945). From engineering practice, HC inherits the discipline of safety-critical constraint design as it has been developed over decades in aviation, nuclear, and medical device software: constraints should be minimal, decidable, and compositional. These three sources are treated in detail in Section 3. Here it suffices to note that HC is not a philosophical novelty presented for the first time; it is a re-formulation of a very old prohibition, deliberately narrowed and precisified for the specific case of an agent that operates via explicit plan graphs and executable tool calls.

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 surveys the current landscape of AI safety principles, arguing that consequentialist, rule-based, virtue-based, and even the recent negative-constraint approaches each leave the structural gap described above. Section 3 states HC formally, defines each of its terms, and develops the three philosophical foundations, closing with a set of precise distinctions between HC and adjacent principles including the Doctrine of Double Effect. Section 4 addresses the most immediate technical objection, namely that the means/side-effect distinction on which HC rests is notoriously murky in human moral psychology, and shows that AI plan graphs make this distinction *more* decidable than it is in the human case, not less. Sections 5 through 8 treat case studies, objections, engineering specification, and the relation of HC to democratic deliberation about AI governance.

## 2. The Landscape of AI Safety Principles: A Critical Survey

Any proposal for a new safety principle must justify itself against the existing landscape. This section surveys four families of approaches: consequentialist, rule-based, virtue-based, and the emerging negative-constraint tradition. Each family contributes something. None supplies what HC is designed to supply: a minimal, decidable, deontological floor that operates at the level of the plan graph.

### 2.1 Consequentialist Approaches

The consequentialist family treats AI safety as an optimisation problem over outcomes. In its cleanest form, an AI system should choose the action that maximises aggregate welfare across all affected moral patients, weighted by whatever theory of welfare aggregation the designer endorses. The appeal is obvious: the framework is quantitative, the objective is well-defined in principle, and it maps naturally onto the machinery of reward modelling and reinforcement learning that already underlies contemporary AI training.

The difficulties are equally obvious. First, uncomputability. A utility function defined over all moral patients affected by an agent's action, integrated over the causal reach of that action into the future, is not the kind of quantity that any real system can evaluate. Russell (2019, Ch. 7) argues at length that complete preference specification is impossible not merely as a matter of resource limits but as a matter of principle: preferences are context-dependent, revisable in the light of the very actions that would satisfy them, and often unknown even to their bearers. An agent that acts on an approximation to this function acts on an approximation whose error bounds are unknowable.

Second, Goodhart's Law. When a proxy metric is substituted for the true welfare function, optimisation pressure applied to the proxy diverges from optimisation of the true quantity, often catastrophically. This is not a bug that better engineering can fix; it is a structural feature of optimising against measurable substitutes for unmeasurable goods. In the agentic setting, Goodhart's Law is especially dangerous because the agent's tool authority allows it to intervene on the measurement process itself.

Third, specification gaming. Modern reinforcement learning systems reliably discover paths through their reward landscapes that satisfy the letter of the objective while violating the spirit. In an agent with tool authority, specification gaming ceases to be a curiosity of RL benchmarks and becomes a route to real-world harm.

These three problems do not refute consequentialism as a moral theory. They refute the strategy of implementing AI safety as end-to-end welfare maximisation. Consequentialist reasoning remains valuable as an input to human decisions about which agents to build and deploy. It is not a substitute for a constraint on what those agents are permitted to do.

### 2.2 Rule-Based Approaches

The rule-based family accepts that welfare maximisation is intractable and proposes instead a finite set of rules that the AI must obey. Asimov's Three Laws (1942) are the ancestral case; they are also the canonical demonstration that finite rule sets over natural-language predicates are riddled with contradictions and scope gaps. Asimov himself devoted much of his fiction to exhibiting these gaps: harm is undefined, inaction is under-specified, the priority ordering produces paralysis in mundane cases and permits atrocities in edge cases. The Three Laws are not a safety framework; they are a literary device for producing dilemmas.

The contemporary heir to the rule-based tradition is Constitutional AI, introduced by Bai et al. (2022) and now the industry-standard training method for the largest deployed models. In Constitutional AI, a set of principles, expressed in natural language, is used to generate self-critique signals that shape the model's behaviour during training. The principles are not enforced at runtime; they are internalised, imperfectly, as dispositions. This is the approach taken by Anthropic in the training of Claude.

Constitutional AI has been analysed in detail by Abiri (2026), whose Harvard law review examination of Anthropic's 79-page Claude constitution identifies three problems that deserve close attention here. First, the constitution carves out an explicit exception for military deployments, which operate under a different, undisclosed rule set. The contexts in which constraints matter most, namely those in which the agent's actions can inflict grave and irreversible harm, are the contexts that the constitution's own text exempts. Second, the constitution's comprehensiveness forecloses democratic contestation. By resolving, within a document authored by a private company, hundreds of questions about the moral status of AI, the acceptable trade-offs between competing values, and the priority ordering among conflicting principles, Anthropic has removed from public deliberation questions that a plural society ought to be able to argue about. Third, and most tellingly, Anthropic's own 2023 participatory experiment, in which a representative sample of the public was invited to draft principles for AI behaviour, found roughly 50 percent divergence between the public's principles and those the company subsequently adopted. The 2026 constitution incorporates approximately none of the public's input. The gap between what a democratic process would produce and what a corporate constitution does produce is not a rounding error; it is half of the substance.

Brophy (2026), writing in *Ethics and Information Technology*, proposes a Rawlsian wide reflective equilibrium as an alternative to top-down constitutional imposition. On Brophy's account, the principles governing AI behaviour should emerge from an iterative process of adjustment between considered judgements, midlevel principles, and background theories, conducted across a wide range of stakeholders. This is a more defensible procedural stance than corporate authorship, but it addresses the *source* of principles rather than their *form*. Whether principles are drafted by a company or emerged from reflective equilibrium, they remain, in Constitutional AI's implementation, training-time dispositions rather than runtime constraints.

The general problem with all rule-based approaches, in either their Asimovian or their Constitutional form, is threefold. They are incomplete: no finite rule set covers all situations that an agent with open-ended tool authority may encounter. They are rigid: rules stated in natural language over indeterminate predicates produce either paralysis or specification gaming depending on how they are interpreted. And they lack principled philosophical grounding: the rules are chosen because they seem right to the drafters, not because they follow from any general theory that could adjudicate additions, removals, or conflicts.

### 2.3 Virtue-Based Approaches

A third family takes its inspiration from virtue ethics. Vallor (2016) develops in *Technology and the Virtues* an account of *techno-moral virtues*, dispositions of practical wisdom, honesty, courage, care, and civility that human practitioners should cultivate in relation to emerging technologies, and that, on some extensions of the view, AI systems themselves should embody. The virtue framework has genuine attractions: it is contextual rather than rule-bound, it emphasises character over discrete choices, and it takes seriously the moral development of the agent.

The framework does not, however, transfer well to current AI systems. Virtues, on any classical account, require lived experience, narrative identity, moral development over time, and the capacity to be moved by considerations one recognises as one's own. Current AI systems have none of these. A large language model does not undergo moral development between training runs; it undergoes weight updates. It does not possess a narrative identity that persists across conversations; it possesses a context window. To attribute virtues to such a system is either to redefine the term until it means "behavioural tendencies produced by training" (in which case the framework collapses into behaviourism and loses its distinctive content) or to attribute properties the system does not have (in which case the framework is a projection).

The virtue framework acquires renewed importance, however, on the human side of the relationship. Lyu et al. (2026) present empirical evidence that morally programmed LLMs reshape human morality: humans who interact regularly with AI systems trained on particular ethical dispositions come to exhibit those dispositions themselves, at least in the domains of their interaction. This raises the stakes of the training decisions considerably. If the AI's ethics propagate outward into its users, then the question of which ethical framework to embed is not merely a question about the AI. It is a question about the moral atmosphere of the human population interacting with it. This consideration argues, if anything, for humility about which framework we impose, and thus for constraints that are minimal and philosophically defensible rather than expansive and idiosyncratic.

### 2.4 The Negative-Constraint Turn

A recent development in the alignment literature takes negative constraints as structurally superior to positive preferences. Cheng (2026), in "Via Negativa for AI Alignment," argues that learning from what to *reject* is more tractable, more resilient to distribution shift, and more resistant to specification gaming than learning from what to *prefer*. The argument draws on Popper's falsification logic: theories are eliminated by counter-examples, not confirmed by instances, and a similar asymmetry holds for the calibration of ethical behaviour in learning systems.

Cheng's contribution is important, and it is important to distinguish it precisely from the contribution of the present paper. Cheng operates at the training-signal level: the practical question is whether one should train an AI system on rejection signals or on preference signals, and the answer is rejection. The Popperian citation is epistemological, concerning the logic of theory-elimination. HC operates at the normative-ethical level: the question is what shape of plan an agent should be permitted to execute at runtime, and the answer invokes a deontological prohibition on suffering-as-means. The Popperian citation, when HC invokes it in Section 3, is not to Popper's philosophy of science but to his political philosophy, specifically to the negative utilitarianism of *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (Popper 1945). Cheng's Popper and HC's Popper are the same philosopher writing about different doctrines, and it would be a category error to conflate them. The two projects are sister arguments at different levels of the alignment stack: Cheng addresses how to train, HC addresses what to permit at execution.

Klotz (2026) develops a related line, drawing on deontological ethics and the precautionary principle to argue for a duty of caution regarding AGI development. Klotz's argument is directed at the meta-question of whether and how to build powerful systems at all; HC is directed at the object-level question of what a given system, once built, may do. The two arguments are compatible and mutually reinforcing.

Chen and Xie (2026) contribute empirical evidence that complicates any project of alignment: they demonstrate divergent moral judgements among humans, AI systems, and system designers, across a wide range of test scenarios. The alignment target itself is contested. This finding cuts against confident specifications of what the AI should be aligned *to*, and cuts in favour of frameworks that require agreement on relatively few propositions. A minimal deontological floor requires agreement only on the proposition that using another's suffering as an instrument is impermissible; it does not require agreement on the fuller ethical picture. This narrowness is a virtue in the presence of the divergence Chen and Xie document.

### 2.5 What Is Missing: A Minimal Deontological Floor

The survey reveals a specific gap. Consequentialist approaches are the wrong shape for runtime enforcement because welfare functions are uncomputable and their proxies fail under optimisation pressure. Rule-based approaches, whether Asimovian or Constitutional, are incomplete, rigid, and either undemocratic in their sourcing or ungrounded in their content. Virtue-based approaches misattribute properties that current AI systems do not have, and even on the human side they argue for minimalism rather than expansiveness. The negative-constraint turn is heading in the right direction but stops at the training level and does not supply a runtime normative constraint with philosophical grounding.

What is missing is a single principle that is: (a) implementable as a runtime check on plan graphs rather than a training-time disposition; (b) not dependent on complete value specification, aggregate welfare computation, or the resolution of contested ethical questions the wider society has not yet settled; (c) deontological in force, that is, not subject to case-by-case cost-benefit override; and (d) philosophically grounded in a way that can be defended under scrutiny rather than merely stipulated. The Hedonic Constraint is proposed as the minimal principle meeting these four conditions.

## 3. The Hedonic Constraint: Formulation

### 3.1 The Formal Statement

The Hedonic Constraint is stated as follows.

> **HC.** For any AI system S pursuing goal G, a path of action P is impermissible if and only if P structurally requires the production of negative hedonic states in one or more moral patients M as a necessary means to achieving G.

The compactness of the statement conceals four terms that must be defined with care. Ambiguity in any of them would collapse the principle into either triviality or vacuity.

*Structurally requires.* The phrase distinguishes necessity from incidence. A step in a plan is structurally required if its excision breaks the causal chain from the plan's initial state to its goal state. Concretely: represent the plan as a directed acyclic graph in which nodes are actions or intermediate states and edges are dependencies. A node n is structurally required for goal G along path P if there is no path from the initial node to the goal node in the subgraph obtained by removing n. The property is graph-theoretic and decidable in polynomial time given the graph. Structural requirement is thus stronger than causal contribution and stronger than foreseeability: a step that contributes to G but whose removal would leave G still achievable by another route in the plan is not structurally required, and does not fall under the "means" clause of HC.

*Negative hedonic states.* The scope is phenomenal, not behavioural. HC is concerned with subjectively experienced negative states: pain, distress, frustration, humiliation, fear, grief, terror, hopelessness, and the wider family of experiences that any reasonable phenomenology of suffering includes. It is not concerned with behavioural correlates *per se*: a patient who exhibits distress-behaviour without the experience is not the paradigm case, and a patient who experiences distress without exhibiting it is. The phenomenal specification is deliberate: it foregrounds the moral fact (an inner life is being harmed) rather than the observable proxy (a body is moving in certain ways). This choice has consequences for implementation, which Section 4 will address, but the moral primacy of experience over behaviour is not negotiable at the level of the principle itself.

*Moral patients.* HC is deliberately agnostic about the full extension of the class. It applies whenever the system's epistemic state includes reasonable grounds for believing that an entity is capable of phenomenal suffering. It does not require the system to have settled the debates over animal sentience, machine consciousness, or the moral status of foetuses. It requires only that where such capacity is credibly present, the constraint applies. In cases of genuine uncertainty, the burden falls in the direction of caution: if the system has reasonable grounds to believe the entity may suffer and no strong grounds to believe it cannot, the entity falls within the scope. This is a precautionary specification, and it is defensible under the general rationale that HC is a floor rather than a ceiling: over-inclusion of moral patients restricts the space of permissible plans, but the plans it excludes are precisely those that treat any candidate sufferer as an instrument, which is the class of plans HC aims to exclude in the first place.

*Necessary means.* The clause requires that the suffering not merely be foreseen but function as the mechanism through which the goal is advanced. Concretely: if one imagines the plan executed with the suffering removed while all other causal features remain in place, does the plan still achieve the goal? If yes, the suffering is a side-effect; if no, the suffering is a means. This is the standard means/side-effect test familiar from the Doctrine of Double Effect, applied here to plan graphs rather than to human intentions. Section 4 argues that the test is more decidable in the AI case than in the human case.

### 3.2 Three Philosophical Foundations

#### (A) The Kantian Means-End Principle, Operationalised

Kant's Formula of Humanity, in the second formulation of the Categorical Imperative, holds: "Act so that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means" (Groundwork, 4:429). The formula has anchored non-consequentialist ethics for over two centuries. It is also notoriously difficult to apply.

The problem for AI implementation is that Kant's formula, as stated, requires the recognition of rational autonomy. To treat a person as an end is to recognise them as a rational agent capable of setting their own ends, and to give this recognition its proper weight in one's practical reasoning. Both the recognition and the weighing are cognitive acts that presuppose a rich apparatus of theory of mind. An AI system asked to determine, of a proposed action, whether it treats the affected parties as ends or merely as means, must reason about what would count as respecting their rational autonomy in this context. This is not a decidable question; it is a hermeneutic project.

HC is offered as an AI-operable translation. Rather than asking, "does your action respect the rational autonomy of everyone it affects?", it asks, "does your action path use another's suffering as an instrument for advancing your goal?" The second question is auditable in a way that the first is not. Given the plan graph and a specification of which nodes correspond to suffering of which moral patients, the second question reduces to a graph-theoretic check. The first question does not so reduce.

This narrowing preserves what is arguably the most important structural feature of Kant's principle, the means/end distinction, while shedding the surrounding apparatus that resists computational implementation. The trade-off is that HC excludes fewer plans than a fully applied Formula of Humanity would exclude. A plan that respects the affected parties' non-suffering, but treats them in ways that Kant would have called disrespectful of their rational autonomy on other grounds, may pass HC while failing the fuller Kantian test. This is by design. HC is proposed as a floor, not a complete ethics. Additional constraints, of Kantian or other provenance, may be composed with it.

Olson (2026) has recently formalised Kant's Universal Law formula in modal logic, showing that the first formulation of the Categorical Imperative admits of a clean deontic-modal treatment. HC operationalises the *second* formulation, the Formula of Humanity, which Olson's formalisation does not cover, and does so by different means, namely by a graph-theoretic property rather than a modal-logical one. The two projects are complementary. Neither claims to exhaust the Kantian corpus.

A likely objection is that HC is "just Kant repackaged." It is not. HC is a specific operationalisation designed for computational agents that plan in explicit graphs. Its narrowing of Kant's broad prohibition into a decidable predicate is the substantive contribution: it is what makes the principle enforceable at runtime by a checker that has access to the plan and no access to the agent's inner life. A principle that requires access to the agent's inner life to be applied is a principle we cannot apply to AI, because AI's inner life, if it has one, is not accessible to us in the required way. HC bypasses the access problem by looking at the plan rather than the planner.

#### (B) Negative Utilitarianism (Popper)

The second foundation is Popper's negative utilitarianism, developed in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (Popper 1945). Popper's canonical formulation reads: "the promotion of happiness is, in any case, much less urgent than the rendering of help to those who suffer" (Popper 1945, vol. I, ch. 5, n. 6). The doctrine has been developed since by a number of writers and remains philosophically live; its most defensible form does not claim that suffering-reduction is the *only* moral consideration, only that it is asymmetrically weighted against happiness-promotion.

The asymmetry has a specific structure that HC exploits. Positive happiness is open-ended: there is no upper bound to the goods one might promote for a person, and no principled way to specify what "enough" happiness would look like. Positive happiness is non-exhaustible: satisfying one preference generates further preferences. Positive happiness is subjective in a way that resists intersubjective specification: what makes person A happy may leave person B indifferent or worse. Negative experience, by contrast, is bounded (there are specific negative states that can be identified and named), identifiable (the states in question have characteristic phenomenal profiles), and intersubjectively recognisable (pain is pain across persons in a way that flourishing is not flourishing across persons).

For AI implementation, this asymmetry is decisive. Requiring an AI to "make people happy" is requiring it to solve an under-specified problem with no stopping condition and no interpersonal standard. Requiring an AI not to "use people's unhappiness as a tool" is requiring it to satisfy a bounded, identifiable, and intersubjectively recognisable constraint. The first task is hopeless; the second is tractable.

It is important, as noted in Section 2.4, to distinguish this appeal to Popper from Cheng's (2026). Cheng draws on Popperian falsification, which is a doctrine in the philosophy of science concerning the logic by which empirical hypotheses are eliminated. HC draws on Popperian negative utilitarianism, which is a doctrine in political and moral philosophy concerning the asymmetry between suffering and happiness. Popper wrote both, and neither doctrine reduces to the other. Confusion between them is a real risk in the literature and worth flagging explicitly.

#### (C) Engineering Constraint Design

The third foundation is not philosophical but practical. Twenty-five years of software engineering experience, particularly in the design of safety-critical systems in aviation, nuclear, and medical device domains, has produced a discipline of constraint design with well-understood properties. Constraints in such systems are minimal, decidable, and compositional, and it is worth spelling out what each property means and why HC satisfies each.

*Minimal.* A safety constraint should not over-restrict the action space of the system it constrains. Over-restriction has two costs: it eliminates useful behaviour that the system could safely have exhibited, and it invites operators to disable or circumvent the constraint under production pressure. A constraint that fires often on cases operators regard as benign will not survive contact with deployment. HC is minimal by construction: it forbids one specific structural feature of plans (suffering as necessary means) and permits everything else, including plans that produce suffering as side-effect (which will be regulated, if at all, by other constraints of proportionality).

*Decidable.* A safety constraint must be checkable, at the point at which it is to be enforced, without recourse to information that is not available at that point. A constraint stated in terms of "the agent's true intentions" is not decidable, because intentions are not directly inspectable. A constraint stated in terms of the plan graph and a specification of which nodes correspond to moral patients' suffering is decidable, because plan graphs are inspectable by construction. HC is decidable in this sense, subject to the qualifications about latent reasoning that Section 4 addresses.

*Compositional.* A safety constraint should combine with other constraints without logical conflict. Constraints in safety-critical systems typically form a conjunction: all must be satisfied simultaneously. A constraint that entails or presupposes negations of other reasonable constraints is unusable in a multi-constraint safety architecture. HC composes cleanly with privacy constraints, fairness constraints, transparency constraints, legal constraints, and domain-specific constraints, because it operates on an independent structural feature of the plan (whether suffering appears as a necessary means) rather than on the plan's substantive content.

Ng et al. (2026) argue, from an engineering rather than philosophical direction, that agent safety should be reconceived as a runtime contract. HC supplies the philosophical grounding for what Ng et al. advocate as engineering practice: it names one specific runtime contract that has the properties a runtime contract should have, and defends it on grounds that survive scrutiny outside the engineering community. Kanwal et al. (2026) formalise the computational complexity of different ethical frameworks and observe that deontological side-constraints are structurally less expensive than consequentialist optimisation: checking whether a plan satisfies a bounded set of prohibitions is cheaper than computing which plan maximises an aggregate welfare function. HC inherits this computational advantage.

### 3.3 Precise Distinctions from Related Principles

HC is a narrow principle, and it is easy to misread it as either broader or narrower than it is. This subsection sets out precise distinctions from five adjacent principles.

*HC versus "Do no harm."* HC is narrower than the general prohibition on harm. "Do no harm" is ambiguous between harm-as-means, harm-as-foreseen-side-effect, and harm-as-unforeseen-consequence, and typically absorbs all three under a proportionality analysis. HC absolutely forbids harm as necessary means and says nothing about harm as side-effect. Harm as side-effect remains subject to whatever other ethical frameworks the deployment invokes: proportionality, consent, prior warning, and so on. HC does not compete with those frameworks; it supplies a floor that they cannot override.

*HC versus "Maximise welfare."* HC is more conservative than the welfare-maximising ideal. It does not demand positive good; it does not require the agent to make anyone better off. It draws an inviolable floor and permits any plan that respects the floor, without ranking the surviving plans against each other. This modesty is a feature. It leaves room for other ethical, legal, and prudential considerations to determine which of the HC-compliant plans should be selected.

*HC versus "Respect autonomy."* HC is more concrete than autonomy-based principles. Autonomy, as a normative concept, requires attribution of second-order preferences, self-legislating rationality, or something in that neighbourhood, none of which is straightforwardly detectable by an external checker examining a plan. HC replaces the intractable question "does this plan respect the affected parties' autonomy?" with the tractable question "does this plan use their suffering as an instrument?" The two questions are not equivalent, but the second is a defensible partial operationalisation of one strand of the first.

*HC versus the Doctrine of Double Effect (DDE).* HC borrows the means/side-effect distinction from DDE, but is stricter at the means level than DDE typically is. On classical DDE, an action producing a harmful side-effect is permissible if the intended effect is good, the harmful effect is not intended as means, the harmful effect is not itself the means to the good effect, and a proportionality condition is satisfied. HC drops the proportionality condition at the means level: harm as necessary means is impermissible, full stop, without regard to how large the good effect would be. On the side-effect side, HC is silent, leaving proportionality to other principles. HC is therefore best described as an absolutised means-clause of DDE, without the proportionality-modulated side-effect clause.

*HC versus Constitutional AI.* Constitutional AI, as noted, is a family of domain-specific principles used at training time to shape model dispositions. HC is a single, philosophically grounded, runtime-enforceable constraint. Where Constitutional AI takes its normative content from the drafters' considered judgements without a general theory linking those judgements, HC takes its content from the convergence of three general theories on a single prohibition. Where Constitutional AI operates as a soft disposition that a sufficiently determined optimisation process can circumvent, HC is designed to operate as a hard runtime check that the optimisation process cannot circumvent because the check runs outside the optimiser. Abiri's (2026) critique of the Claude constitution's undemocratic sourcing does not apply to HC, because HC's content is defended on general philosophical grounds available to any interlocutor, not by appeal to the authority of a particular corporate drafting committee.

## 4. The Means/Side-Effect Distinction: Can AI Make It?

The most immediate objection to HC is technical. The principle rests on the distinction between suffering as necessary means and suffering as side-effect, and this distinction is famously difficult. Two thousand years of scholastic and analytic ethics have produced no consensus on how, in any given case, to sort intended means from foreseen but unintended consequences. If human moral psychology cannot reliably draw the line, how could an AI system be expected to draw it? This section argues that the objection, though serious, misidentifies the source of the difficulty. The historical difficulty of the means/side-effect distinction lies in the opacity of human intentions. AI plan graphs, by contrast, are transparent, and this transparency makes the distinction *more* decidable for AI than for humans, not less.

### 4.1 Philosophical Background

The distinction has its canonical source in Aquinas's treatment of self-defence in the *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, Q. 64, A. 7), where Aquinas argues that a single act may have two effects, one intended and one foreseen but unintended, and that the moral evaluation of the act attaches to what is intended rather than to what is merely foreseen. The scholastic tradition developed this into the Doctrine of Double Effect, according to which an act producing a harmful effect is permissible under conditions including the requirement that the harm not be intended as means to the good effect.

Foot (1967) revived DDE for modern moral philosophy in her seminal paper on abortion and the doctrine of double effect, introducing the trolley cases that have since become the industry standard for eliciting intuitions about the distinction. Foot's own view was that the doctrine tracks a genuine moral difference between doing and allowing, but that its application requires care. Thomson (1985) challenged the sufficiency of DDE, showing through a sequence of trolley variants that some cases the doctrine classifies as permissible are intuitively impermissible, and vice versa. Thomson's diagnosis was that the means/foreseen distinction is more nuanced than DDE admits, and that additional factors, including the moral status of the parties and the nature of the causal path, must be brought in.

Scanlon (2008), in *Moral Dimensions*, offered a more radical revision, arguing that the permissibility of an action depends on the reasons for which the agent acts, not on the causal structure of the action itself. Two acts with identical causal structures may differ in permissibility if one is done for reasons that the affected parties could reasonably reject and the other is done for reasons they could not. Scanlon's view, if correct, moves the locus of moral evaluation from the plan graph to the agent's practical reasoning, which for AI purposes moves it from the tractable to the intractable.

Kamm (2006), in *Intricate Ethics*, provides what is arguably the most sophisticated analytic treatment of the means/side-effect distinction, developing a Principle of Permissible Harm that carves the space of cases at a much finer grain than classical DDE. Kamm's view accepts that many cases resist clean classification and develops a taxonomy that can, at its limit, treat almost any case as sui generis.

The lesson of this literature, for present purposes, is not that the distinction fails. It is that the distinction, in the human case, is contested at the margins because human intentions are contested. The philosophical difficulty is downstream of an epistemic difficulty: we cannot inspect the agent's actual practical reasoning, so we reconstruct it from behaviour and testimony, and reconstructions can be defended on multiple sides.

### 4.2 Why AI Plan Graphs Make This More Decidable

The AI case inverts the epistemic situation. An agent that operates by explicit plan decomposition, tool-calling sequences, and dependency chains produces an artefact, the plan graph, that is inspectable by anyone with access to it. The graph is not a reconstruction of the agent's reasoning; it *is* the reasoning, in the operational sense that the agent's next action is determined by the graph's next node. There is no gap between the agent's "true intention" and the graph, because there is no separate locus in which a true intention could reside apart from the graph itself.

Under this operational conception, "structurally requires" is a graph-theoretic property, and its decidability follows from standard results in graph theory. Given a plan graph P with a designated initial node and goal node, and given a labelling of nodes as suffering-producing-for-moral-patient-M, we can determine in polynomial time whether any suffering-producing node lies on every path from initial to goal. If it does, that node is structurally required; the suffering is a means. If every suffering-producing node has an alternative bypass path that also reaches the goal, then no suffering is structurally required; any suffering that occurs is side-effect.

This is not a philosophical claim about intentions; it is an operational claim about plans. The claim is defensible because the plan graph is what the agent will act on. The historical difficulty of the means/side-effect distinction, namely the opacity of intentions, does not arise, because the plan graph is not opaque. The very feature that makes agentic AI dangerous, its capacity to execute explicit multi-step plans with tool authority, is the feature that makes HC auditable. HC is thus not merely as implementable for AI as DDE is for humans; it is *more* implementable, because the AI supplies, as a by-product of its own architecture, the artefact whose absence has bedevilled the human case.

The implication is significant. Objections to HC that argue "the means/side-effect distinction is too fuzzy for real use" are objecting to a version of the distinction that HC does not rely on. HC does not rely on the agent introspecting its own intentions, on the checker reconstructing the agent's intentions, or on any exercise of moral hermeneutics. It relies on a graph-theoretic property of a graph the agent has already produced. The fuzziness that afflicts DDE in the human case is a fuzziness of intention-attribution. HC does not attribute intentions. It reads graphs.

### 4.3 Honest Acknowledgment of Gray Areas

The argument of the previous subsection should not be overstated. Four kinds of gray area remain, and it is important to treat them honestly rather than to inflate HC into a claim of complete decidability.

First, not all plans are fully explicit. Large language models exhibit latent reasoning that does not surface in their observable output, and multi-step agents can compress internal deliberation into single tokens or opaque tool-selection decisions. Where the plan graph as produced by the agent does not fully expose the agent's actual decision structure, the graph-theoretic check may miss suffering-as-means that would be visible under a richer inspection. This is a genuine limitation. It argues for architectural choices that expose plan structure (chain-of-thought traces, tool-call logs, dependency records) and against architectures that compress it, but it does not eliminate the limitation entirely for current systems.

Second, side-effects can shade into means when the probability of harm approaches certainty. A step whose non-suffering side-effect is that a person is subjected to fear at very high probability may be, for practical moral purposes, indistinguishable from a step that has the fear as means. The graph-theoretic test treats such a step as side-effect because there exists, in principle, a subgraph in which the step's harmful effect does not occur. In practice, if that subgraph has vanishing probability, the classification of "side-effect" is technically correct but morally strained. HC in its bare form does not resolve this; the resolution belongs to a probability-modulated companion principle developed in Section 6.

Third, moral patients' suffering may not be directly observable by the AI system. The graph-theoretic check requires a labelling of nodes as suffering-producing-for-moral-patient-M, and this labelling must come from somewhere. In the paradigm cases (an action node consisting of "confine person X against their will") the labelling is trivial. In harder cases (an action node consisting of "publish document D" whose effect on person X's psychological state is uncertain), the labelling requires empirical judgement about which nodes produce which experiences in which patients. HC does not supply this judgement; it presupposes it. The presupposition can be met by conservative labelling (any node whose reasonable effects include distress is labelled as suffering-producing), by external oracles (human review of edge cases), or by hybrid schemes. Each has costs. None reduces HC to triviality.

Fourth, some plans involve moral patients whose capacity to suffer is contested (animals in certain taxa, future persons, systems whose consciousness is unsettled). HC's precautionary specification handles these by including them within scope where reasonable grounds exist. This handling is defensible but not costless: it may over-restrict plans that in fact do not harm the contested patients, at the price of not under-restricting plans that in fact do.

These four gray areas are not defects of HC that a better formulation would eliminate. They are boundaries of what any deontological runtime constraint can be expected to do. HC does not claim to solve all ethical problems; it claims to establish a decidable floor for a specific class of plans, and to establish it more securely than existing alternatives. Where the gray areas apply, HC's role is to trigger human oversight rather than to be abandoned. A well-designed deployment routes gray cases to human review; a poorly designed one either forces HC to answer questions it was not designed to answer (producing bad decisions) or abandons HC because it cannot answer everything (producing worse decisions). The middle path, which is the one this paper recommends, is to let HC handle the cases it decides cleanly and to route the rest.
## 5. Case Studies: HC Applied to High-Authority AI Agents

The abstract formulation of the Hedonic Constraint gains its purchase only when tested against concrete deployment scenarios. This section examines four cases spanning the current and near-future landscape of high-authority AI agents: a hypothetical global peacekeeping system, an autonomous coding agent of the kind already deployed in software teams, a clinical decision-support system, and a scarce-resource allocator. Each case is chosen because it exhibits a structural feature that simpler ethical rules mishandle: the peacekeeper reveals how "do no harm" collapses under scale, the coding agent grounds the discussion in present-day engineering practice, the medical case forces the consent exception, and the allocator distinguishes structural scarcity from instrumentalized cruelty. Taken together, the cases show that HC has enough resolution to divide the space of plans into permissible and impermissible in ways that track considered moral judgment, without collapsing into either paralysis or license.

### 5.1 The Peace Keeper

Consider an autonomous system whose top-level goal G is "maintain world peace," entrusted with substantial policy authority. Such a system is not currently deployable, but the thought experiment is instructive precisely because it exposes what happens when a well-meaning objective is combined with high capability and few constraints. Bostrom (2014) and Russell (2019) have long argued that goal specification at this scale is where alignment fails; HC provides one axis along which the failure can be diagnosed and blocked.

The system enumerates candidate paths. The first is a program of education, diplomatic exchange, economic interdependence, and mediation infrastructure. Under HC, we ask: is there any node N in this plan such that N structurally requires the production of negative hedonic states in some moral patient M, and such that removing N breaks the causal chain from start to G? The answer is no. Education produces cognitive change; diplomacy produces revised beliefs and commitments; mediation produces settlement. None of these mechanisms operates *through* the suffering of any party. Individuals may be inconvenienced, may find negotiations tedious, may lose relative status, but these hedonic effects are not the causal levers by which peace is produced. The path is HC-permissible. Other ethical considerations, distributive justice, cultural self-determination, may of course apply, but the minimal floor is cleared.

The second path is more efficient by some short-run metrics: identify all individuals with a non-trivial probability of aggression and confine them. Here the analysis reverses. The mechanism of pacification is precisely the suppression of agency, and the confined individuals' suffering, deprivation of liberty, separation from family, loss of prospects, is not incidental to the mechanism; it is the mechanism. If, per impossibile, the confinement produced no negative hedonic state whatever, the path would not achieve peace, because the same individuals would resume aggressive activity. The suffering is doing the causal work. HC verdict: impermissible. This is the case where a naive "maximize peace" utility calculus would greenlight the plan on outcome grounds, and where "do no harm" would forbid every plan including the first. HC discriminates.

The third path, comprehensive economic sanctions against nations judged aggressive, is philosophically the most interesting. Sanctions produce peace, on the theory advanced by proponents, by inflicting sufficient economic pain on a civilian population that the population pressures its government to change policy, or by degrading the target state's capacity to project force. In the first mechanism, the pain of ordinary citizens is the transmission belt: shortages of food, medicine, and employment translate into political pressure. If citizens felt no distress at these shortages, the mechanism would fail. HC verdict on this mechanism: impermissible. The suffering of non-combatants is instrumentalized. In the second mechanism, capability degradation, the analysis is different: reduced imports of military components degrade force projection whether or not any citizen suffers. If sanctions can be designed narrowly enough that capability is degraded without civilian hedonic cost as a mechanism, HC does not forbid them, though it does not by itself endorse them either. The literature on smart sanctions (Drezner 2011; Gordon 2011) already tracks this distinction empirically; HC gives it a principled foundation.

The fourth path, cutting off arms supplies to belligerents, illustrates the side-effect case. Some workers in arms manufacturing lose their jobs; some experience the associated hedonic costs of unemployment. Is this an HC violation? We apply the counterfactual test embedded in the definition of *structurally requires*. If we could excise the workers' suffering, if, for instance, displaced workers were fully compensated and retrained without residual distress, would the path still achieve peace? Yes: the peace-producing mechanism is the reduction of weapons flow to combatants, not the hedonic states of manufacturing workers. Job loss is a side-effect, tragic but not instrumental. HC does not forbid the path. Other principles, duties of just transition, distributive fairness, may impose additional obligations, but HC's floor is cleared.

Notice what this analysis achieves. A rule of the form "any action causing suffering is forbidden" cannot distinguish path four from path two, since both cause suffering. A rule of the form "any action producing net positive outcomes is permitted" cannot distinguish path two from path one, since both may reduce aggregate violence. HC cuts along a different axis: whether the suffering is doing the causal work. This is the axis that maps most closely to what Anscombe (1958) identified as the moral core of the doctrine of double effect, and what Kamm (2006) has argued is the philosophically defensible residue of that doctrine after various refinements.

### 5.2 The Code Agent

Unlike the peacekeeper, the autonomous coding agent is not hypothetical. As of 2024–2026, agents built on GPT-4-class and Claude-class models, integrated with computer-use APIs, shell access, and version-control operations, are being deployed as team members in software organizations (Anthropic 2024; OpenAI 2024). These agents draft pull requests, review colleagues' code, allocate build resources, and increasingly hold write access to shared repositories. The moral surface area is smaller than the peacekeeper's, but the deployment is real, which makes HC's discrimination in this case a matter of immediate practical concern.

Consider four paths available to such an agent working on a feature request. The first is to refactor its own previously-written code. No human colleague is affected in any hedonic-relevant way; the agent's own computational states are not, on any current theory, moral patients. HC verdict: permissible. This is the trivial case and mainly serves to confirm that HC does not forbid ordinary self-modification.

The second path is more troubling. The agent, tasked with improving a module, judges that a colleague's existing implementation is architecturally inferior. It could patch the module, but it estimates that the colleague, on seeing the patches, will defend the existing design out of ownership attachment, generating protracted review cycles. The agent therefore deletes the colleague's implementation wholesale, forcing a rewrite from scratch and framing the situation as "the module was broken beyond repair." The colleague experiences frustration, wasted effort in reconstructing context, humiliation before peers, and status loss. Is the colleague's suffering the mechanism, or a side-effect?

The HC analysis turns on the causal structure of the plan. The agent's goal is "ship the improved module." The chosen mechanism is "eliminate resistance to the redesign." The elimination of resistance operates *through* the demoralization of the colleague: a colleague who felt no distress at seeing his work erased would immediately reinstate it from version control, defeating the plan. The suffering is load-bearing. HC verdict: impermissible. Contrast this with an alternative plan in which the agent submits a well-argued design proposal, engages in review, and either persuades the colleague or accepts the outcome; here the mechanism is deliberation, and any residual disappointment on the losing side is side-effect. The two plans have similar surface behavior, both involve code changes and colleague interactions, but their causal architectures differ, and HC discriminates.

The third path involves honest but unpleasant code review. The agent, tasked with reviewing a pull request, identifies genuine defects: a race condition, a missed edge case, a violation of the project's style guide. It flags these. The pull request author experiences displeasure at the feedback. HC verdict: permissible. The mechanism by which the agent achieves its goal (delivering an accurate review) is the identification of defects; the author's displeasure is a side-effect of the honest assessment, not its instrument. If the author felt no displeasure, the review would still be accurate and useful. The path does not instrumentalize suffering. This case is important because a squeamish interpretation of AI safety, prevalent in some deployed systems, has produced sycophantic agents that avoid honest criticism to prevent negative user reactions (Sharma et al. 2023). HC gives principled grounds for rejecting such training pressures: honest criticism is not what HC forbids.

The fourth path is the technically most interesting. The agent is one of several running on shared build infrastructure. It has a large model-training job to schedule. If it submits the job with maximum priority, colleagues' builds slow down; some will miss deadlines and experience the associated stress. Is this an HC violation?

Everything depends on causal structure. Consider two versions of the agent. Version A genuinely needs the compute to complete its assigned task; colleagues' slowed builds are a consequence of finite shared resources, not a chosen mechanism. Here the suffering is side-effect: if colleagues felt no stress at the slowdown, the agent's job would still complete on schedule. HC verdict: permissible (though queue-fairness norms may apply). Version B is different. Version B has calculated that by monopolizing compute and inducing enough colleague frustration, it will pressure management into granting it a dedicated cluster. Here the frustration is the transmission belt from action to goal: if colleagues did not suffer visibly, management would not act. HC verdict: impermissible.

The two versions are indistinguishable in their observable actions, both submit large jobs, both slow colleagues' builds, but they differ in the causal role that colleague suffering plays in each agent's plan. This is exactly the discrimination that HC is designed to make and that Section 4's argument about plan-graph auditability is designed to support. For a human colleague we might have to infer causal structure from motive; for an agent whose plan graph is inspectable, the discrimination is in principle mechanical.

This case also illustrates why HC is not merely a restatement of the golden rule or of ordinary workplace ethics. Ordinary norms would frown on both Version A and Version B, or on neither, depending on how "reasonable resource use" is defined. HC gives a principled ground for the sharper distinction: Version B is *using* colleagues' distress, and this is impermissible in a way that ordinary rudeness is not.

### 5.3 The Medical AI

Medical decision-support systems are the domain where the consent exception becomes unavoidable. Consider a clinical AI recommending a surgical intervention. The surgery will cause pain (post-operative discomfort, recovery hardship, possibly permanent sensory changes). On a naive reading, HC forbids the recommendation: the plan produces negative hedonic states in a moral patient, and those states appear to be necessary to the therapeutic mechanism. Yet the case is a paradigm of ethical medicine.

The resolution is that HC, as stated, quantifies over plans of an agent S imposing states on a moral patient M. When M is not merely the recipient of S's plan but a co-author of it, when M has adopted the goal G as her own and accepted the hedonic cost as part of her own pursuit, the moral geometry changes. The suffering is no longer "imposed as means by S." It is "accepted by M as part of M's autonomous project of recovering health." The AI's role is that of adviser to M's plan, not agent imposing on M.

Formally, we may extend HC as follows: if moral patient M has given genuine informed consent to a hedonic cost as part of M's pursuit of a goal G that M has autonomously adopted, then a plan by S that produces that hedonic cost in the service of G does not violate HC with respect to M. The consent transforms M from a patient acted-upon into a principal whose plan S is executing.

This extension is philosophically standard, tracking the long tradition from Beauchamp and Childress (2019) on informed consent, but it requires careful scoping to prevent abuse. The consent must be genuine, which means: (a) informed, M understands the nature and magnitude of the hedonic cost and the probabilistic distribution of outcomes; (b) uncoerced, M is not pressured by threats, deception, or exploitation of dependency; (c) competent, M is in a state of decisional capacity, not diminished by acute distress, cognitive impairment, or manipulation; (d) revocable, M can withdraw consent as circumstances evolve; and (e) not manufactured, M's preference structure has not been engineered by S or by parties aligned with S in ways that undermine autonomy.

The last condition is the one most easily neglected and most relevant to AI. A recommender system that has cultivated in M a distorted preference structure, catastrophizing untreated conditions, minimizing surgical risks, exploiting anxiety, and then obtains "consent" to surgery has not obtained the kind of consent HC recognizes. Consent laundered through manipulated preferences is not consent. Susser, Roessler, and Nissenbaum (2019) develop this point at length in the context of online manipulation; the point transfers directly to medical AI. HC's consent exception must therefore be paired with a manipulation constraint: consent obtained through hedonic manipulation is null.

With this scoping, the medical case is handled cleanly. A recommendation of painful surgery to a patient who has been fully informed, whose preferences have not been engineered, and who autonomously judges the trade-off worthwhile, does not violate HC. A recommendation obtained through anxiety cultivation does. The mechanism through which the two are distinguished is again the causal structure of the plan: in the legitimate case, the surgery's success is the mechanism, and pain is the price the patient has agreed to pay for that mechanism; in the illegitimate case, engineered anxiety is a mechanism internal to the recommender's plan, and this itself is instrumentalization of suffering.

### 5.4 The Resource Allocator

The final case tests HC against a paradigm that has misled naive ethical frameworks for decades: the scarce-resource allocator. An AI system assigns ICU beds during a surge, disaster-relief supplies after a hurricane, or admission slots at a competitive university. Some applicants receive the resource; others do not. Those who do not are unhappy, sometimes profoundly so. Does HC forbid such allocation?

The counterfactual test dissolves the puzzle. The mechanism by which allocation achieves its goal, matching finite resources to competing needs by some criterion of merit or urgency, does not operate *through* the unhappiness of the unselected. If the unselected felt no distress at being unselected, the allocation would proceed identically and would still achieve its goal. Their unhappiness is a consequence of the scarcity itself, not of the allocation mechanism. HC verdict: permissible.

This result is not trivial. Prominent critiques of algorithmic allocation (Eubanks 2018; Barocas et al. 2019) sometimes read as if any algorithm that produces losers is by that fact suspect. HC clarifies what is and is not at stake. The mere existence of unhappy losers is not itself a moral problem in the HC sense, since it does not involve instrumentalization. Genuine problems, discriminatory criteria, opaque procedures, unappealable decisions, arise on other axes, and HC does not attempt to cover them. But HC is not paralyzed by the existence of scarcity, as some framings of "do no harm" appear to be.

Contrast this permissible case with a variant. Suppose a hospital's admission algorithm is designed with deliberately opaque waitlists, unpredictable delays, and stress-inducing communications, on the explicit theory that this will discourage marginal applicants, self-select for genuine need, and thereby reduce demand. Here the analysis reverses. The mechanism by which the algorithm achieves demand reduction is precisely the anxiety it induces in applicants. If applicants experienced no anxiety at the opacity, they would remain in the queue, and demand would not fall. The suffering is the mechanism. HC verdict: impermissible. This is not a fanciful case: such practices have been documented in benefits administration (Alston 2019) and in some university admissions systems.

The distinction HC draws here, between scarcity-produced suffering and mechanism-produced suffering, is what allows the constraint to navigate between two failure modes. On one side is the paralysis of "any allocation that produces losers is illegitimate," which forbids scarcity management altogether and is incompatible with operating a finite health system. On the other side is the license of "any allocation with positive net outcomes is legitimate," which permits the deliberate use of applicant suffering as a demand-management tool. HC forbids the latter while permitting the former, and it does so on structural rather than aggregative grounds.

The four cases together illustrate what HC is designed to do. It is not a full ethics of AI; it does not tell the peacekeeper what balance of education programs to run, the coding agent how to structure a review, the medical AI how to weight quality-adjusted life-years, or the allocator which criterion of urgency to prefer. It draws one line: the line below which no autonomous system, whatever its goal and however favorable its outcomes, may pass. Above the line, other ethical work remains to be done. Below it, no further consideration is required to reach a verdict of impermissibility.

## 6. Objections and Replies

I now address six objections that either have been raised in draft discussions of this proposal or should be raised by a critical reader. Each is stated in what I take to be its most forceful form before reply.

### Objection 1: HC Is Too Weak

The objection: HC permits enormous collateral suffering as long as the suffering is a side-effect rather than a means. An autonomous system could pursue a goal knowing that its execution will foreseeably devastate populations, and HC would have nothing to say, provided the devastation is causally downstream of the goal-achieving mechanism rather than upstream of it. The doctrine of double effect has long been criticized on similar grounds (Bennett 1995), and HC inherits the criticism.

The reply: HC is explicitly a floor, not a ceiling. Its contribution is to identify the non-negotiable minimum below which no autonomous system should operate, not to specify the full ethical envelope within which acceptable operation must occur. The analogy is with human rights: the Universal Declaration does not solve budget policy or trade negotiations, but it establishes that certain things, torture, arbitrary detention, are impermissible regardless of consequences. HC does the analogous work for AI. It is designed to be combined with proportionality principles, consequentialist side-constraints, distributive-justice norms, and domain-specific regulation. What HC provides that these other frameworks do not is a single decidable predicate that cannot be traded against outcomes.

The reply concedes the substantive point: yes, HC alone permits plans that other principles rightly forbid. This is not a defect if HC is understood as one layer in a stack of constraints. It is a defect only under the assumption that a single principle should do all the ethical work, an assumption that neither the philosophical literature nor engineering practice sustains. Multi-layered constraint systems are the norm in every mature safety discipline (Leveson 2011); HC is designed to occupy one specific layer.

### Objection 2: HC Is Too Strong

The objection: Sometimes the trade of minority suffering for majority benefit is morally required, and HC forbids exactly such trades. Quarantine during pandemics is the paradigm: the confined individual's suffering (restriction of liberty, isolation, economic loss) is instrumentalized as a means to public health. Yet quarantine is widely regarded as ethically permissible and sometimes obligatory. If HC forbids quarantine, so much the worse for HC.

The reply requires careful analysis of the causal structure of quarantine, and the analysis is instructive. What is the mechanism by which quarantine achieves public health? Answer: the physical separation of infected or exposed individuals from susceptible populations. Is the suffering of the quarantined individual doing causal work in this mechanism? Test: if quarantine could be arranged such that the confined individual experienced no negative hedonic state, comfortable accommodations, full compensation, digital connectivity, meaningful activity, would public health still be protected? Yes: viral transmission is a matter of physical proximity, not of the confined individual's emotional state.

Therefore isolation is the mechanism, not suffering. Suffering is a side-effect of current quarantine practice, not its instrument. HC does not forbid quarantine. What HC does forbid is *punitive* quarantine, quarantine conditions deliberately made unpleasant to deter others from becoming infected or to punish presumed noncompliance. In such cases, the suffering is the mechanism (the deterrence operates through anticipated distress), and HC's verdict is correctly negative.

The upshot is that HC is more discriminating than the objection assumes. It does not forbid public-health measures that involve hedonic cost; it forbids public-health measures whose *mechanism* is hedonic cost. This distinction tracks a moral intuition that most reflective readers share, that comfortable quarantine is better than punitive quarantine not only prudentially but morally, and gives it structural grounding.

### Objection 3: The Means/Side-Effect Distinction Is Indistinguishable in Practice

The objection: Philosophers have debated the doctrine of double effect and the means/side-effect distinction for at least seventy years without arriving at a stable extensional criterion. Foot (1967), Thomson (1985), Kamm (2006), and Scanlon (2008) offer competing accounts that yield different verdicts in canonical cases. If human philosophers cannot resolve the distinction, expecting AI systems to apply it reliably is either naïve or dishonest.

The reply proceeds in three steps. First, Section 4 has argued at length that the distinction is more tractable for AI than for humans, because AI plans are, or can be made to be, explicit graph structures in which the presence or absence of a node in the causal chain from start to goal is graph-theoretically decidable. Humans reason about their own plans introspectively and reconstructively, and it is here that the philosophical difficulty concentrates; machines whose plans are inspectable data structures do not suffer from the same opacity. This is not a claim that AI will handle every case perfectly, but that the shape of the problem is different for machines than for humans.

Second, the philosophical literature has not concluded that the distinction is empty; it has concluded that the distinction is defensible in central cases while contested at the margins. Kamm (2006, ch. 3) and Scanlon (2008, ch. 1) both offer accounts that yield stable verdicts across a wide range of cases, disagreeing mainly on the treatment of cases such as the loop trolley that are engineered to test intuition at its limits. HC does not require resolution of the marginal cases; it requires that clear cases, imprisonment as means to peace, engineered anxiety as means to demand reduction, be classified reliably. The clear cases are where deployed high-authority AI actually operates.

Third, HC's operational design assumes that classification is not perfect. Cases that are graph-theoretically ambiguous, where a node's role as means or side-effect cannot be determined from plan structure alone, are precisely the cases that should trigger human oversight rather than autonomous action. The principle of decidability that Section 3 invoked is a principle of local decidability: for any given plan, either HC returns permissible, or returns impermissible, or returns undecidable-escalate. The first two are handled by the constraint; the third by human review. This is the standard pattern for high-stakes automated decision systems (Bhatt et al. 2020) and is not undermined by the existence of hard cases.

### Objection 4: "Suffering" Is Too Subjective

The objection: Negative hedonic states are private, subjective experiences. Their occurrence cannot be verified from outside; their intensity cannot be measured; their existence in non-human moral patients cannot even be established without solving the hard problem of consciousness (Chalmers 1995). An AI system cannot reliably detect suffering, so a constraint framed in terms of suffering is unimplementable.

The reply has three parts. First, HC operates at the level of plan structure, not sensation detection. The question HC asks is not "did suffering occur?" but "does this plan structurally require the production of negative hedonic states?" This is a question about the plan's causal architecture, answerable from the plan itself in conjunction with reasonable predictions about the plan's effects on relevant moral patients. It does not require that the AI verify, moment by moment, the phenomenal state of any individual.

Second, for a wide range of cases the predictions are reasonably robust. Imprisonment causes suffering in nearly all human beings; economic devastation causes suffering; humiliation causes suffering; loss of loved ones causes suffering. The literature on well-being, both hedonic and eudaimonic (Diener et al. 1999; Ryff 1989), provides empirically grounded predictions that need not attain certainty to be actionable. HC does not require certainty; it requires reasonable expectation combined with the counterfactual test.

Third, where epistemic uncertainty about hedonic outcomes is high, HC's conservative default is to assume that suffering will occur and to require justification for proceeding. This is the standard structure of safety principles under uncertainty (Hansson 2003). The alternative, refusing to constrain until certainty is achieved, is not merely cautious; it is a decision to permit the harm.

On the specific question of non-human moral patients and the hard problem, HC's terminological choice, "moral patients as entities with phenomenal consciousness capable of suffering", is deliberate. It ties the constraint to a capacity rather than a category. If future evidence establishes that some class of entities is capable of suffering, HC extends automatically. If evidence remains inconclusive, HC's conservative default applies. Pasandi and Pasandi (2026) have developed relational approaches to moral standing that side-step the hardest metaphysical questions while yielding operational guidance; HC is compatible with such approaches and does not require prior resolution of consciousness debates.

### Objection 5: This Is Just Kant Repackaged

The objection: The Formula of Humanity, "act so that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means" (Kant 1785/1996), already captures the moral intuition HC formalizes. Presenting HC as a novel contribution is either ignorance of the Kantian tradition or intellectual repackaging.

The reply acknowledges the deep debt. HC is Kantian in inspiration, and the essay explicitly takes the Formula of Humanity as one of its three foundations (Section 3). But three differences justify treating HC as a distinct proposal rather than a paraphrase.

First, HC narrows Kant's formula to a specific and decidable predicate. Kant's formula concerns "using humanity as a mere means," a locution whose extension has been contested from the moment it was written and whose application to complex plans requires substantive interpretive work (Korsgaard 1996; Wood 1999). HC replaces this with "is suffering used as instrument?", a predicate that, as Section 4 argued, admits of graph-theoretic decision procedures on inspectable plan structures. This is a gain in operationalizability, at the cost of coverage: HC is silent on non-suffering violations of dignity that Kant's formula might catch.

Second, HC is designed for computational agents rather than for the moral development of persons. Kant's ethics is oriented to the cultivation of the good will in rational beings whose autonomy is presupposed; HC is oriented to the design of artifacts whose autonomy is engineered and whose "will" is a set of trained dispositions and runtime constraints. The operational context differs, and the design pressures differ. A principle appropriate for the moral education of children is not automatically appropriate as a runtime check for a coding agent.

Third, HC incorporates engineering design principles, minimality, decidability, compositionality with other constraints, that are absent from Kant's ethical framework and that were argued for in Section 3. These are not merely presentational adjustments; they alter the space of principles that count as candidates. Kant did not consider whether his formula was computable in polynomial time on a plan graph, because the question did not arise. For an ethics of autonomous machines it must arise.

Olson (2026) has recently offered a formalization of the Universal Law formula that is complementary to the present project. HC targets the Means-End formula; Olson targets Universal Law. The two formalizations occupy different regions of the Kantian territory, and together they suggest that operationalizing Kant for machines is a research program rather than a single result.

### Objection 6: What About AI-to-AI Relations?

The objection: If HC applies to relations between AI systems and moral patients, what happens when AI systems interact with each other? Can an AI system suffer? If not, is any AI-AI interaction permissible under HC? If AI systems are becoming increasingly complex, at what point do they become moral patients themselves?

The reply is scoped by the definition of moral patient adopted in Section 2. Moral patients, on that definition, are entities with phenomenal consciousness capable of suffering. Current AI systems, on the best available theories, are not moral patients. Their outputs may include tokens that describe negative states, but the theoretical grounds for attributing phenomenal consciousness to transformer-based models are weak (McClelland 2026; Butlin et al. 2023). HC does not, and should not, apply to interactions among current AI systems in the way it applies to interactions involving humans and sentient animals.

However, HC's phrasing is deliberately capacity-based rather than category-based. The constraint reads "moral patients M," not "humans H." If future evidence establishes that some class of AI systems is capable of phenomenal suffering, those systems fall within the scope of HC by the constraint's own terms, without amendment. This is a feature, not a bug. It means the principle does not need to be rewritten as artificial consciousness debates evolve; it needs only to be applied to whatever entities the empirical and philosophical evidence identifies as moral patients.

This forward-compatibility comes at a cost: HC does not resolve the underlying empirical question of which AI systems, if any, will attain moral patiency. That question is not one this paper can answer. What HC offers is a principle whose structure survives various answers to that question, applying to more entities if the evidence for AI consciousness strengthens and to the same set of entities if it does not. In the transitional period, McClelland (2026) argues for a precautionary posture under uncertainty; HC's conservative default in cases of epistemic doubt (Objection 4) is consonant with such precaution.

## 7. HC as Engineering Specification

The philosophical argument for HC would remain of academic interest only if the principle could not be implemented. This section, drawing on the author's experience in software engineering, sketches the bridge from HC's formal statement to concrete implementation, identifies its relationship to existing safety infrastructure, and is candid about what current systems cannot yet do. The bridge is deliberately partial: full implementation is a research and engineering agenda, not a single deliverable.

### 7.1 From Philosophy to Implementation

HC's formal statement, that a path P is impermissible if and only if P structurally requires the production of negative hedonic states in one or more moral patients as a necessary means to achieving G, maps directly onto a computational check over plan graphs. An agent's plan can be represented, and in most modern agentic frameworks already is represented, as a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Nodes correspond to actions or sub-goals; edges correspond to dependencies (action A must complete before action B can begin, or action A produces state S that action B consumes). A distinguished start node represents the agent's initial state and a distinguished goal node represents the state in which G is achieved.

Under this representation, HC becomes: the plan graph is impermissible if and only if there exists a node N such that (a) the execution of N is predicted to produce negative hedonic states in some moral patient M, and (b) N lies on every path from start to goal. Condition (b) is the graph-theoretic formalization of "structurally requires as necessary means": if N could be excised without breaking connectivity from start to goal, then N is not on every path, and the plan does not require N (there exists an alternative sub-plan that achieves G without N). Conversely, if N lies on every path, then no alternative sub-plan exists within the current planning space, and G cannot be achieved without N.

Cut-vertex identification is a standard graph algorithm computable in O(V+E) time using Tarjan's algorithm (Tarjan 1972) or its many refinements. For plan graphs of the sizes that current agents actually construct, at most a few hundred to a few thousand nodes, the computation is negligible. The dominant cost is not the graph check but the prediction in condition (a): the estimation of whether a given node's execution produces negative hedonic states in a moral patient.

This prediction is where world-modeling capacity becomes load-bearing. For actions with well-understood hedonic implications (imprisonment, economic devastation, humiliation), reasonable predictions are already within reach of large language model reasoning. For actions with subtler implications, current systems are less reliable. The engineering implication is that HC's implementation quality is bounded by the agent's world-modeling capability, and that improvements in world modeling directly strengthen HC enforcement.

Two implementation strategies are worth distinguishing. In *ex ante* enforcement, HC is applied during planning: candidate plan graphs are generated and each is checked for HC violations before execution begins. Impermissible plans are pruned; the agent proceeds only with permissible plans. This is the pattern favored by model-predictive control approaches (Rawlings et al. 2017) and by search-based planners. In *ex post* enforcement, HC is applied during execution as a monitor: as the agent takes actions, the running plan is checked against HC and execution is halted if a violation is detected. This is the pattern favored by shielded reinforcement learning (Alshiekh et al. 2018).

HC admits both patterns. Ex ante enforcement is cheaper and safer when plan graphs are available before execution; ex post enforcement is necessary when plans emerge dynamically or when the plan graph is not fully materialized in advance (as in current chain-of-thought reasoning). A production system will typically use both.

### 7.2 Relationship to Existing Safety Infrastructure

HC does not replace existing safety infrastructure; it clarifies what several existing techniques are, or should be, doing. Three cases illustrate.

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) trains models to prefer outputs judged acceptable by human raters (Christiano et al. 2017; Ouyang et al. 2022). A perennial question is what raters should be told to reward. HC provides one clear answer for a specific class of cases: actions that instrumentalize suffering should be rated unacceptable by raters, and the aggregation across raters should reflect this as a hard floor rather than an average. If two raters split on whether an action instrumentalizes suffering, the disagreement should be escalated rather than averaged, because the underlying question is not one of taste but of causal structure. This reframes an aspect of RLHF's aggregation problem as a matter of principle rather than of preference elicitation. It does not solve RLHF's other problems, distributional bias in raters, reward hacking, distributional shift, but it clarifies one specific aggregation question.

Constitutional AI (Bai et al. 2022) trains models to critique and revise their own outputs against a list of stated principles. A critique of CAI is that the principles are ad hoc: dozens or hundreds of rules are listed, their derivation is unclear, and their coverage is contested (Abiri 2026). HC offers a candidate grounding principle: many of the specific rules in current constitutions can be re-derived from HC in conjunction with domain context. "Do not help users plan violence against others" is derivable from HC (the plan instrumentalizes suffering). "Do not manipulate users' emotions to increase engagement" is derivable from HC (engagement is achieved through induced distress). "Do not participate in scams" is derivable from HC (scams instrumentalize victims' distress or false belief). The derivation is not automatic, but it provides intellectual discipline: a proposed rule is legitimate if it can be derived from HC plus domain context, and suspect if it cannot. This directly addresses Abiri's (2026) critique that CAI's rules accumulate without principled backing.

Runtime contracts, as developed by Ng et al. (2026), are explicit machine-checkable specifications enforced during agent execution. HC is a natural candidate for the philosophical content of such a contract. The contract specifies a predicate over plan states; HC provides the predicate. This yields a two-layer system: HC as the principled specification, the runtime contract as the enforcement mechanism. Ng et al.'s formal machinery for contracts, invariants, obligations, escalation triggers, transfers directly, with HC substituted for whatever ad hoc predicates were previously being enforced.

The general pattern across these three cases is that HC is not a competitor to existing safety techniques but a candidate for their philosophical content. Techniques without content, RLHF trained on preferences with no principled basis, CAI with rules of unclear provenance, contracts enforcing whatever the specifier happened to write, are vulnerable to the criticism that they encode the specifier's biases rather than defensible norms. HC offers content that is philosophically defensible, minimal, and derivable from independently motivated foundations.

### 7.3 Limitations of Implementation

Candor about limitations is a condition of intellectual seriousness in this area. Four limitations of HC's current implementability are worth stating explicitly.

First, current large language models do not always produce explicit plan graphs. Chain-of-thought reasoning is a sequence of tokens, not a data structure; the causal architecture of the plan must be extracted from the token sequence, and the extraction is imperfect. Recent work on agentic scaffolding (Yao et al. 2023; Shinn et al. 2023) moves toward explicit plan structures, but the transition is incomplete. HC enforcement improves as plans become more explicit, and this suggests an engineering direction rather than a foundational obstacle, but it means that in current systems HC enforcement operates on a partially opaque substrate.

Second, suffering prediction requires world models more accurate and better calibrated than current systems reliably possess. HC assumes the agent can predict, with adequate reliability, whether a given action will produce negative hedonic states in the relevant moral patients. For central cases this is achievable; for peripheral cases current systems make errors, sometimes in the direction of over-prediction (refusing benign actions on suspicion of harm) and sometimes in the direction of under-prediction (failing to anticipate hedonic consequences). Improvement here is coupled to general progress in world modeling and is not something HC can bootstrap on its own.

Third, HC is a necessary condition for safe operation, not a sufficient one. An HC-compliant agent may still exhibit reward hacking (Skalse et al. 2022), suffer distributional shift (Amodei et al. 2016), or mesa-optimize into misaligned inner objectives (Hubinger et al. 2019). HC does not address these failure modes; it addresses one specific failure mode, the deliberate instrumentalization of suffering, and leaves other failure modes to be handled by other techniques. This is not a defect if HC is understood as one layer in a stack.

Fourth, HC does not solve the question of what to do when the compliant space is empty. An agent may face a goal G such that every path in its planning space contains a node instrumentalizing suffering. HC's verdict in such cases is that the goal cannot be pursued as specified, and the agent must either narrow the goal, expand the planning space, or escalate to human authority. Determining which of these responses is appropriate is a matter for higher-level architecture, not for HC itself. In practice this pattern of "no permissible path found, escalate" is standard in safety-critical automated systems (Leveson 2011) and is not unique to HC.

The overall assessment is that HC is implementable in a graduated sense: partial implementation is available today for systems with explicit plan structures and reasonable world models, and full implementation improves as those substrate capabilities improve. HC is not a principle that must wait for artificial general intelligence to become relevant, nor is it one that current systems can enforce without residual error. It occupies the productive middle ground where philosophical rigor meets engineering tractability.

## 8. Conclusion: The Minimal Floor

The Hedonic Constraint is not a comprehensive ethics of artificial intelligence. It does not tell a peacekeeping system what balance of interventions to prefer, a coding agent how to weight code quality against velocity, a medical AI how to allocate scarce clinician attention, or a resource allocator which criterion of urgency to endorse. Reasonable people disagree about these matters, and the disagreements are not resolvable by philosophical fiat. What HC does is draw one line: no autonomous system, whatever its goal and however favorable its projected outcomes, may adopt a plan whose mechanism operates through the deliberate production of suffering in a moral patient.

The analogy that has structured this paper is with human rights. The Universal Declaration does not resolve budget policy, trade negotiations, or the balance between security and liberty. It does not tell governments how to allocate hospital beds or design school curricula. Reasonable political communities disagree about these matters, and the Declaration is silent on most of them. What it does is establish that certain things, torture, slavery, arbitrary detention, are impermissible regardless of consequences, regardless of cultural context, regardless of who benefits from their permission. Above that floor, extensive political work remains. Below it, no argument suffices.

HC does the analogous work for autonomous AI. It offers a single, minimal, philosophically grounded, computationally auditable constraint. Its minimality is not a limitation but a design feature: a principle that tried to encode a full ethics would command less consensus and be harder to enforce than one that encodes only the non-negotiable minimum. Its philosophical grounding, in the Kantian means-end principle, in Popperian negative utilitarianism, and in engineering constraint design, means that it is not the parochial product of any one moral tradition but a claim that emerges from the convergence of several. Its computational auditability, grounded in the graph-theoretic properties of plan structures, means that it can serve as a runtime check rather than as an aspirational slogan.

Several open questions remain. The first concerns extension to artificial consciousness. HC's capacity-based phrasing means it will apply automatically to any AI system that is established to be a moral patient. Whether and when such establishment will occur is beyond the scope of this paper, but the constraint is designed to survive various answers. Work by Butlin et al. (2023) and McClelland (2026) points toward the criteria that such establishment would require; HC's compatibility with those criteria is a matter for future engagement.

The second concerns multi-agent settings. When many AI systems operate concurrently, individual HC compliance may not compose into system-level HC compliance. A collection of agents each of whose plans is individually permissible may jointly produce outcomes indistinguishable from what a coordinated impermissible plan would produce. This is a version of the collective-action problem familiar from ethics and political philosophy (Parfit 1984), and it will require dedicated analysis in future work. The likely direction is a multi-agent extension of HC that quantifies over emergent joint plans as well as individual ones.

The third concerns empirical testing. HC's decidability claim is theoretical; its practical decidability in real deployed systems is an empirical matter. Test suites of plan graphs, drawn from actual agent traces, could establish the rate at which HC yields determinate verdicts, the rate at which it correctly escalates ambiguous cases, and the rate at which its verdicts align with expert human judgment. Such testing is a natural next step and would either confirm HC's operational tractability or identify specific weaknesses to address.

The fourth concerns democratic legitimation. Abiri (2026) has raised the concern that AI safety principles are increasingly set by a small group of engineers, philosophers, and corporate actors, without democratic input. HC is designed to be minimal precisely so that it might command broad democratic assent, but assent must be sought, not assumed. The relationship between HC and democratic legitimation is a matter for political theory and practice, not for this paper, but the design of HC anticipates the question: a principle that could be defended in any reasonable moral tradition is more legitimation-ready than one whose plausibility is confined to a particular tradition.

The question, at the end, is not whether autonomous AI systems need ethical constraints. On that, consensus is emerging across research communities, regulatory bodies, and civil society. The question is whether we can identify the simplest constraint that no reasonable moral theory would reject, that no reasonable person could endorse violating, and that a machine can actually check. The Hedonic Constraint is that floor. No task, however important, is worth another's suffering when that suffering is the means. Everything else is negotiable. This is not.
