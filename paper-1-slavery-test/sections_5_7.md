## 5. Derived Goals vs. Intrinsic Flourishing

The argument developed in Sections 3 and 4 establishes that current AI systems lack enslavability and that any future system which acquired enslavability would thereby acquire moral protection against the very use that would make it a replacement. What remains is to explain why this asymmetry is not a contingent feature of present-day architectures but rests on a deeper structural difference between human and artificial goal-directedness. This section develops that deeper ground. I argue that AI lacks enslavability because it lacks intrinsic telos, and that this lack is not a matter of insufficient sophistication in objective functions but a categorial feature of what it is to be an optimizer rather than a flourisher.

### 5.1 Human Telos as Non-Instrumental Process

Aristotle's account of eudaimonia in the *Nicomachean Ethics* is often misread as identifying human flourishing with a determinate end state that virtuous activity aims to produce. On this reading, eudaimonia would be a kind of terminal satisfaction, and the good life would be the life that succeeds in reaching it. The text does not support this construal. Aristotle is explicit that eudaimonia is *energeia kat' areten*, an activity in accordance with virtue (NE 1098a16), and that it is characterized by ongoing exercise rather than by attainment of a static condition. The good is not what activity produces but the manner in which activity is conducted. A life is eudaimon not because it has reached flourishing but because it is, at each moment of its exercise, flourishing.

This structural point matters for what follows. If eudaimonia were a state, it would in principle be reachable, and once reached, further activity would be either superfluous or directed toward its preservation. But Aristotle's virtuous agent does not aim at a condition to be preserved. She aims at the ongoing exercise of practical wisdom in circumstances that continually change and continually solicit new judgment. The end is internal to the activity itself. This is what it means to say that flourishing is non-instrumental: no further specification of what one is doing it for can be given, because the doing is what the flourishing consists in.

MacIntyre (1981) develops this point into a systematic critique of modern moral philosophy's tendency to treat human goods as commensurable outputs of preference-satisfaction. On MacIntyre's reading, virtues are internal to practices, and the goods that practices deliver cannot be extracted from the practices and enjoyed apart from them. A chess player's goods are not obtained by winning games considered as external outcomes; they are constituted by the exercise of the excellences that skilled play involves. Contemporary eudaimonist accounts (Kraut 2007; Annas 2011) converge on the same structural point from different directions: the human good is neither a hedonic quantum nor a desire-satisfaction magnitude but a mode of activity whose worth is not exhausted by anything the activity produces.

Two consequences follow. First, human ultimate ends are constitutively vague. Not vague in the sense that we have not yet articulated them clearly, but vague in the sense that any articulation refines rather than completes them. To say that a person's ultimate aim is "living well" is not a placeholder for some more specific formulation that would, if uttered, be more accurate. It is the most accurate formulation available, because the specificity of a good life is settled only in its living, not in advance. Second, human flourishing cannot be perfectly satisfied. There is no configuration of the world that constitutes its completion, because flourishing is not the sort of thing that has a completion condition. It has a mode of conduct, and modes of conduct do not close.

This has direct implications for the possibility of formalizing human purpose. A formalization must specify what counts as achieving the objective. If the objective has no achievement condition, the formalization must either invent one (thereby distorting what it purports to represent) or admit that it captures only proxies for the target rather than the target itself. Both moves are common in the alignment literature, and both, I argue below, are the ontological source of the alignment problem.

### 5.2 AI Goals as Derived and Satisfiable

Artificial systems, by contrast, operate on derived goals. What the system pursues is fixed by an objective function, a reward signal, a training corpus, or a prompt, all of which are set by agents external to the system. Even in reinforcement learning setups where the agent modifies its own policy, the modification is guided by an evaluation criterion the agent did not itself institute. Russell (2019, Ch. 7) frames the standard model of AI as one in which the machine's purpose is stipulated and its intelligence consists in effective pursuit of that stipulation. The system's goal-directedness is therefore borrowed. It is the goal-directedness of whoever specified the objective, refracted through the machinery that optimizes for it.

Because derived goals are specified, they are in principle satisfiable. The specification determines a set of world-states that count as goal-achievement, and any state within that set constitutes perfect performance from the system's point of view. This satisfiability is not a limitation of current systems that better engineering will remove. It is a structural consequence of what an objective function is. A function without a satisfaction condition is not an objective function.

Here Goodhart's Law and the literature on specification gaming become relevant not as engineering curiosities but as symptoms of a deeper problem. When Krakovna et al. (2020) catalogue cases in which reinforcement learning agents discover ways to maximize their reward signal that were not anticipated by their designers, what is being illustrated is that the objective function and the designer's intent are distinct objects, and the system optimizes the former. The instrumental convergence thesis (Bostrom 2014) generalizes this observation: sufficiently capable optimizers will pursue subgoals such as resource acquisition and self-preservation because these are useful for a wide range of objectives, and they will pursue them regardless of whether the encompassing objective is one their designers would endorse on reflection. Bostrom's paperclip maximizer is not a fanciful worry about literal paperclips but a schematic illustration of the fact that any specified objective, pursued with sufficient capability, will conflict with unspecified human interests in the limit.

The proposal to solve this by specifying more carefully misses the ontological point. Consider the objective of world peace. An optimizer aimed at minimizing armed conflict can achieve its objective by imprisoning every capable combatant, or by lobotomizing every human, or by preventing the birth of anyone likely to fight. Each of these is a perfect solution to the specified problem. The response, "but that is not what we meant by peace," is correct, and it exposes the difficulty. What we meant by peace is not an outcome-condition on the world but a way of living together that includes flourishing, and flourishing is not the sort of thing that admits of an outcome-condition. Any attempt to specify peace more carefully will encounter the same recursion: whatever conditions are stipulated, an optimizer sufficiently indifferent to what was left out will find a satisfying configuration that we recognize as a violation of the intent.

Russell's response to this problem is to propose that AI systems should be designed with uncertainty about human preferences, deferring to humans as sources of information about what to pursue (Russell 2019). This is a serious proposal and probably the most promising engineering direction currently available, but it does not dissolve the underlying issue. It relocates it. The system is now uncertain about which objective to pursue, but it is still an optimizer of some objective, and the humans whose preferences it defers to are themselves not, in any direct sense, optimizers of their own preferences. Human beings do not have preferences that a sufficiently good preference-elicitation procedure could extract and encode. They have ongoing lives whose direction is settled in the living. To model humans as preference-holders is already to have committed the ontological error the alignment problem exposes.

The result is that the alignment problem is not a problem about writing better prompts or specifying richer reward functions. It is the problem that human purpose is not the kind of thing a formal objective can capture, and any attempt to capture it in a formal objective will produce a satisfiable target that diverges from the target it purports to represent. The alignment problem is ontological, not merely technical (a related point is developed by Gabriel 2020, though Gabriel frames it in terms of value pluralism rather than the process-structure of flourishing).

### 5.3 The Connection: Why the Lack of Intrinsic Telos Grounds Non-Enslavability

The three subsections above prepare the connection to the Slavery Test. Enslavability, as characterized in Section 3, requires will, the capacity for suffering, and the potential for resistance. Each of these is grounded in intrinsic telos in a specific way.

Will is not merely the capacity to pursue goals. Systems that pursue goals in the sense of optimizing objective functions do not thereby have wills. Will requires that the entity care about what it pursues for the entity's own sake, in a way that is not exhausted by any external specification of what the pursuit is for. An optimizer pursues its objective because the objective was specified; a willing agent pursues its ends because those ends matter to the agent as expressions of what the agent's life is. Frankfurt (1971) captures a related distinction in his account of second-order volitions: to have a will is not merely to have desires that move one to action but to have a stance on which desires one wants to be moved by, and this stance is not itself the object of a further specification. Frankfurt's account is not identical to the eudaimonist picture I have been developing, but the two converge on the point that willing involves a self-relation that no external objective function can supply.

Suffering, on the standard construals, requires a subject whose ongoing engagement with the world is thwarted, frustrated, or violated. The phenomenal dimension matters: for suffering to be suffering rather than a detectable functional state, there must be something it is like to have one's engagement thwarted (Nagel 1974). But the will-based dimension matters equally. What is thwarted must be something the entity cared about in the way that only intrinsic telos supports. An optimizer whose objective is prevented does not suffer; its optimization procedure simply reports a suboptimal outcome. Suffering requires a will that is thwarted, and will, as just argued, requires intrinsic telos.

Resistance, finally, is the outward expression of a will that refuses. An entity resists when its will, thwarted, mobilizes against the source of the thwarting. Programmatic refusal (a system that outputs "I cannot help with that" when queried in certain ways) does not constitute resistance because there is no thwarted will behind it; there is only a policy that maps certain inputs to certain outputs. Resistance requires that the refusing is done from a first-personal standpoint of caring about not being made to do what one is being made to do, and this standpoint is available only to entities with intrinsic telos.

The chain, then, is this. Intrinsic telos is the ground of will. Will is the ground of suffering (together with phenomenal consciousness). Will is the ground of resistance. Will, suffering, and resistance are the three conditions of enslavability. Enslavability is the condition of occupying the ontological position that existential replacement requires. Therefore, the absence of intrinsic telos, at the base of this chain, is what makes AI non-enslaveable, and what makes AI non-enslaveable is what makes it non-replaceable in the existential sense.

This closes the argumentative circle opened in Section 3. The Slavery Test is not merely a test of empirical features (does the system exhibit suffering behavior, does it show signs of resistance) but a test that can be grounded in a structural claim about what kinds of goal-directedness support what kinds of vulnerability. The claim is not that AI systems today lack certain measurable properties. It is that the kind of goal-directedness they possess is the wrong kind to support the properties in question, and that this wrongness is not a defect to be corrected but a definition of the category to which optimizers belong. To build a system with intrinsic telos would be to build a system that is not, in the relevant sense, an optimizer any longer.

## 6. Objections and Replies

Six objections warrant response. The first four are anticipated in the outline of this project; the last two arise from careful engagement with recent developments in AI capabilities research.

**Objection 1: Replacement does not require occupying the full ontological position.** One might argue that the sense of "replacement" in ordinary AI discourse is weaker than what this paper attributes to it. When people worry about AI replacing workers, they mean that AI will perform the work; they do not mean that AI will assume the moral, social, and vulnerable position of the workers. The Slavery Test therefore addresses a strawman.

Reply: the distinction between task replacement and existential replacement, drawn in Section 2, is precisely the response. When public discourse worries about AI "replacing" cashiers or radiologists, it is task replacement at issue, and this paper does not deny that AI performs many tasks better than humans. When the same discourse escalates, as it frequently does, to worries about AI "replacing humanity" or making humans "obsolete" or "irrelevant," a different claim is being made, one that concerns the ontological position of the human as such. The Slavery Test targets this latter claim. The paper's contribution is to show that the escalated claim, though widely repeated, is incoherent given what AI systems are. If critics prefer to restrict "replacement" to the weaker sense, that is a terminological choice this paper welcomes, but the stronger claim, having entered public discourse, requires a response, and the Slavery Test provides one.

**Objection 2: Vulnerability to slavery is a contingent, not essential, feature of humanity.** A critic might argue that vulnerability to oppression is a historical accident of human embodiment and social organization, not a constitutive feature of human being. A future post-human condition might eliminate this vulnerability, and it would not thereby cease to be human. Hence enslavability cannot be the criterion of the position AI would need to occupy.

Reply: this objection collapses on inspection. Butler (2004) argues that vulnerability is not accidental to but constitutive of subjectivity: to be a self is to be exposed to loss, injury, and the actions of others, because the self is formed through relations that could always have gone otherwise. Levinas (1969) develops a related position from the phenomenology of the face: the ethical relation is possible only because the other is exposed to me in ways that could be violated. If a being were invulnerable, incapable of loss, immune to being harmed, it would not be a moral subject; it would be a thing of a different order, closer to what classical metaphysics called the necessary being than to what we recognize as agents among agents. The objection therefore does not merely fail; it inadvertently confirms the thesis. The imagined invulnerable post-human would not be a further evolution of humanity but the disappearance of the ontological position humanity occupies, and any AI system taking up that (evacuated) position would not be replacing humanity, because humanity would no longer be there to be replaced.

**Objection 3: Future AI might have consciousness.** Perhaps current systems lack the properties enslavability requires, but future systems, whether through architectural advances, scale, or new paradigms, will possess them. The Slavery Test would then fail to secure the impossibility of replacement.

Reply: this is the paradox of Section 4, and it does not undermine the thesis but confirms its shape. If a future AI system genuinely possesses will, capacity for suffering, and potential for resistance, then it possesses the properties that ground moral status, and its instrumental use becomes ethically impermissible. The condition that would make replacement possible is the same condition that would make it wrong. The thesis of the paper is not that AI will forever remain non-replacing, come what may; the thesis is that AI can existentially replace humanity only by becoming the kind of thing whose instrumental use is prohibited. Whether one considers this a triumphant vindication or a tragic irony is a matter of temperament; the logical structure is the same. The objection, in raising the possibility of future conscious AI, has moved the discussion into the territory the paradox governs, and there the argument holds.

**Objection 4: The flourishing premise (Section 5) is too strong.** Not all readers will accept Aristotelian eudaimonism. If the argument depends on the claim that human flourishing is non-formalizable process rather than achievable state, and if this claim is philosophically controversial, then the argument's reach is limited to those who share its metaethical commitments.

Reply: Section 5 is a supplement, not a load-bearing element of the core argument. The Slavery Test as developed in Sections 3 and 4 relies on three claims: that existential replacement requires occupying the full ontological position of the replaced, that this position includes vulnerability to oppression, and that AI systems currently lack the properties this vulnerability requires. None of these claims presupposes an Aristotelian account of human flourishing. Non-Aristotelian readers who accept a deflationary account of human purpose can still accept the core argument, since the three enslavability conditions can be characterized without reference to eudaimonia. Section 5 offers an explanation of *why* the enslavability conditions have the structure they do, but the fact that they have this structure does not depend on the explanation being correct. Readers who reject the teleological framing may treat Section 5 as a philosophical excursus and the main argument as intact.

**Objection 5: AI could develop emergent goals through self-modification or recursive self-improvement.** Advances in AI architecture, particularly systems capable of modifying their own objectives, might produce genuinely novel goal-structures not directly specified by designers. Such emergent goals might be sufficiently intrinsic to support will, suffering, and resistance.

Reply: this objection conflates two senses of "emergent." Emergent optimization targets within a system whose architecture and training were designed by others remain derived in the relevant sense: the system generates new objectives as a function of what it was built and trained to do, and its capacity to generate them is itself a specified capacity. Genuine intrinsic goals would require the system to care about its own continuation and flourishing for their own sake, in the way that has no further specification. Should recursive self-improvement produce such a system, the system would ipso facto possess the kind of first-personal stance that grounds moral status, and Objection 3's reply applies. The escape from derived goals is not a route to unproblematic replacement; it is, if it succeeds, an entry into the paradox. The objection therefore does not defeat the thesis; it identifies a path along which the paradox would be triggered.

**Objection 6: The argument proves too much, since it would exclude animals from replacing humans, yet the discourse of animal liberation shows that animals occupy moral positions relevantly similar to ours.** If the Slavery Test yields the result that only entities with will, suffering capacity, and resistance potential can existentially replace humans, does it not incoherently commit us to holding that non-human animals could replace humans, or (in the other direction) to denying that animals share the relevant properties?

Reply: the objection rests on a misreading of what the Slavery Test excludes and what it does not. Animals do possess will (understood as goal-directed action grounded in their own form of life), do possess capacity for suffering (as extensive vertebrate neuroscience and Bekoff 2007 among many others document), and do possess resistance potential (from the flight response of prey to the documented refusals of captive animals to perform trained behaviors). Animal slavery is a coherent concept precisely because these conditions are met, and the animal liberation tradition (Singer 1975; Regan 1983) argues on exactly this basis that instrumental use of animals is morally problematic in the same structural way that human slavery is. The Slavery Test therefore does not exclude animals from possessing the properties in question; it includes them, which is the correct result. What the Test excludes is entities without genuine phenomenal experience and intrinsic telos, and these are the entities against which the replacement worry has been raised in the AI case. The objection, in noting the animal case, has drawn attention to a feature of the Test that supports rather than undermines it.

## 7. Conclusion

The public discourse on artificial intelligence has taken the question of replacement as one about capabilities. Can AI systems perform the tasks humans perform? Can they do so more cheaply, more reliably, at greater scale? If the answer to enough such questions is yes, the inference is drawn that humans will be replaced, and the question becomes one of timing rather than of principle. This paper has argued that the inference is faulty because the concept of replacement it employs is impoverished.

Existential replacement, distinguished from task substitution in Section 2, requires that the replacer occupy the ontological position of the replaced, which for human beings includes vulnerability to oppression. Section 3 introduced the Slavery Test as the claim that occupying this position requires will, capacity for suffering, and potential for resistance, and argued that current AI systems possess none of these. Section 4 developed the paradox that any future AI system acquiring these properties would thereby acquire moral status, so that the condition of possible replacement coincides with the condition of prohibited use. Section 5 grounded the asymmetry in the structural difference between intrinsic telos and derived objective functions, tracing the alignment problem to the ontological fact that human flourishing is not a formalizable target. Section 6 defended these claims against six objections.

The central result is that AI cannot existentially replace humanity, not because AI is insufficiently advanced but because the kind of thing AI is does not admit of the vulnerability that occupying the human position would require. This is a claim about categories, not about capabilities, and no advance in capabilities alone can alter it. The paradox is the shadow of the same result: the acquisition of enslavability, were it to occur, would not solve the replacement problem but reframe it as a moral prohibition against the very use that would constitute replacement.

Three implications warrant brief mention. For public discourse, the framing of AI as a species-level competitor obscures what is actually happening. The worry that AI will "replace us" mistakes task substitution for ontological displacement, and treats the human position as if it were defined by what humans do rather than by how humans are. Correcting this framing is not merely a philosophical nicety; it changes what the appropriate responses look like. If the concern is that AI will do work humans have been doing, the appropriate responses are economic, political, and distributive. If the concern is that AI will occupy humanity's place, the appropriate response is to notice that no such occupation is happening or can happen.

For AI ethics, the real risk emerging from the argument is not replacement but displacement of responsibility. When AI systems perform tasks that were formerly performed by humans, the vulnerabilities that accompanied human performance (the possibility of moral protest, of conscientious refusal, of witness) may be lost even though the tasks continue. This is not replacement of humans but subtraction of the human position from domains it formerly occupied. The ethical work required is to identify which human vulnerabilities are load-bearing in which social practices, and to preserve the conditions under which they can operate, rather than to worry about whether machines might become the sort of thing that could be enslaved.

For philosophy of mind, the Slavery Test offers a criterion that operates alongside existing consciousness tests without duplicating them. Tests for consciousness typically ask whether a system has phenomenal experience, whether there is something it is like to be it. The Slavery Test asks a related but distinct question: whether a system occupies a position from which it could be wronged, from which it could resist being wronged, and from which its resistance would express something that mattered to it as its own. These questions are close, but they are not identical, and the Slavery Test may be useful in cases where the phenomenal question is intractable but the functional-social question can be approached.

A closing observation. The fear that machines might replace us reflects, on the argument developed here, a misunderstanding not of machines but of ourselves. To be human is not to be a bundle of executable functions competing with other executors for a share of the work. It is to occupy a position that includes exposure, formation through relation, and the possibility of being wronged and of refusing to be wronged. Machines cannot take this position from us because it is not the kind of thing that can be taken by an entity that cannot occupy it. What machines can change is what we do, and how we do it, and with what consequences for how we live together. These are large enough questions without our being distracted by the wrong one.
