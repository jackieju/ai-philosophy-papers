# The Slavery Test: On the Impossibility of Existential Replacement by Artificial Intelligence

**Weihua Ju**, *Independent Researcher*

---

## 1. Introduction

The question of whether artificial intelligence will "replace" humanity has become a fixed point in both public discourse and academic philosophy. The last three years have intensified this question in ways that older debates about automation did not anticipate. Large language models now draft legal briefs, compose functioning software, plan multi-step projects, and hold extended conversations that competent readers cannot reliably distinguish from human writing. Autonomous coding agents such as OpenAI's Codex and Anthropic's Claude with tool use can decompose ill-specified goals into subtasks, execute those subtasks against external systems, and revise their plans in light of intermediate results. Bostrom's (2014) hypothetical superintelligence, once treated by many philosophers as a distant thought experiment, has been retrofitted as a description of near-term systems. The framing question has correspondingly hardened: given the trajectory of capability gains, at what point, and in what respect, will these systems displace the human beings who now occupy various social and economic positions?

I want to argue that this framing, which I will call the capability framing, misses what is philosophically at stake in the replacement question. The capability framing treats replacement as a function of task-performance parity: if system S can perform every task T that person P performs, at comparable or superior quality, then S can replace P. This is the implicit structure behind most contemporary discussions of automation risk, whether the discussants are optimists (Floridi 2023) or pessimists (Russell 2019; Bostrom 2014). Even Hannah Arendt's (1958) prescient warnings about a society freed from labor without knowing what to do with its freedom take the transfer of tasks as the driving mechanism, however much she resisted reducing the human condition to the executor of those tasks. The capability framing is not wrong so much as it is answering a different question. It answers the question of substitution. It does not answer the question of replacement.

This paper defends a distinction between task substitution and existential replacement, and it argues that the latter, but not the former, is what people are actually worried about when they ask whether AI will replace humanity. The distinction is ontological, not merely rhetorical. Substitution occurs when one thing performs a function previously performed by another; replacement occurs when one thing occupies the full ontological position previously occupied by another. A washing machine substitutes for a launderer with respect to the function of washing clothes. It does not thereby replace the launderer as a member of a household, a wage earner, a citizen, a person with a family, a bearer of rights, or a being whose suffering matters. Nothing in the machine's competent execution of the washing function transfers any of these dimensions of the launderer's position to the machine. The launderer, upon acquiring a washing machine, is not replaced; he or she is, at most, freed from one task.

The recent Topoi symposium on the ethical paradox of automation (2025) frames the same insight in a different register: automation generates ethical predicaments precisely because it dissociates functional replaceability from moral replaceability. A system that can do the work of a caregiver, without being able to bear any of the moral weight the caregiver bore, does not thereby lift that weight; it merely relocates it. My argument in this paper generalizes the Topoi observation into a formal criterion, which I call the Slavery Test.

The Slavery Test asks: could this candidate replacer be enslaved by those who currently exercise power over the replaced? The question is deliberately harsh, but the harshness is diagnostic rather than provocative. Historically, when one group of human beings has been said to "replace" another in the sense at issue, when one people has moved into the fields, workshops, and households of another, the mechanism of replacement has almost invariably been enslavement. Enslavement is the extreme case of forced occupation of another's ontological position, and it is the case that reveals what such occupation requires. It requires a being with a will that can be broken, a capacity to suffer that can be exploited, and a potential for resistance that must be suppressed. These are not accidental features of the enslaved; they are what makes enslavement possible in the first place, and, as I will show, what makes replacement possible more generally.

The core inference of the paper is that non-enslaveability entails non-replaceability in the existential sense. If a candidate replacer cannot be enslaved, because it has no will to break, no capacity to suffer, and no potential to resist, then it cannot occupy the ontological position of the being it purports to replace. It can only execute the tasks that being once executed. Current AI systems, I argue, fall on the non-enslaveable side of this line. Their goals are externally specified, not internally generated; their operations lack the phenomenal properties that consciousness studies now converge in treating as the marks of subjective experience (Butlin et al. 2023; Schwitzgebel 2026); and their apparent refusals are programmatic constraints on output, not the exertion of a violated inner will (Northoff and Gouveia 2024). None of this is a bet on the long-term trajectory of AI research. It is an assessment of what current systems, however capable, actually are.

The argument then generates a paradox, which I take to be independent of any particular empirical bet about AI's future. If AI systems ever come to satisfy the three conditions for enslaveability, they will thereby have acquired what is, on any plausible account of moral status (Singer 1975; Floridi 2023; Chalmers 2023), the ground of moral consideration. The moment they can replace human beings in the existential sense is the moment we ought not permit them to be used as replacements, because the very features that make them replacers are the features that make their instrumental use morally impermissible. Replacement-capacity and moral-protection-capacity are, on this account, co-instantiated. They are not two separate properties that happen to correlate; they are the same underlying capacity described from two different normative angles.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 develops the ontological distinction between substitution and replacement, drawing on Arendt's (1958) trichotomy of labor, work, and action, and on more recent phenomenological work (Coeckelbergh 2022; Soeffner 2023) that has tried to specify what belongs to a human position beyond the tasks that fill it. Section 3 presents the Slavery Test proper. It develops the three conditions for enslaveability, drawing on Patterson (1982) and Hegel's master-slave dialectic (Hegel 1807/1977, §178-196), argues that AI systems currently satisfy none of them, and states the core inference from non-enslaveability to non-replaceability. Section 4 develops the paradox of acquirable enslaveability and shows that it is a logical rather than merely ethical structure. Later sections address the deeper question of whether the impossibility here rests on a still more fundamental fact about the difference between derived goals and intrinsic flourishing, and consider objections to the argument.

Two clarifications before proceeding. First, the argument is not a claim about what AI cannot in principle become; it is a claim about the logical structure of replacement. If AI acquires the relevant properties, the argument still holds, but its consequence changes from an impossibility result to a moral prohibition. Second, the argument is compatible with substantial concern about the social effects of AI-driven automation. Substitution has enormous consequences; it can immiserate workers, deform institutions, and undermine democratic life. The claim here is not that these consequences are trivial. It is that they are not consequences of replacement, and that framing them as such produces both bad philosophy and bad policy. What is happening in the actual world is task substitution at scale, with all the political and economic problems that follow from it. Existential replacement, in the sense the phrase seems to promise, is not happening, and, on the argument to follow, cannot happen while the replacer remains what current AI is.

## 2. Replacement versus Substitution

The distinction I want to press is between two relations that a system S can bear to a person P. In the substitution relation, S performs some function F that P had performed, such that after S's introduction P need not, or cannot, perform F. In the replacement relation, S comes to occupy the position that P occupied, such that after S's introduction P is no longer required as the occupant of that position at all. Substitution is a relation between actions; replacement is a relation between occupants of ontological positions. The two relations can be dissociated, and their dissociation is what the capability framing obscures.

Consider first a case where the two relations coincide only apparently. A firm employs a bookkeeper; it acquires accounting software that performs, faster and more accurately, the calculations the bookkeeper performed; it dismisses the bookkeeper. The software has substituted for the bookkeeper's function. Has it replaced the bookkeeper? The bookkeeper is no longer employed, and the position "bookkeeper" may no longer appear on the firm's organizational chart, but nothing has taken over the bookkeeper's position as a person. The bookkeeper still eats, sleeps, votes, mourns, and matters morally to those around her; she has lost a job, not been replaced. The software occupies no position at all in the fabric of social existence; it is a piece of infrastructure, like a filing cabinet or a photocopier, that the firm now owns and uses. That the firm's owner may misdescribe the situation, saying that the software "replaced" the bookkeeper, is telling but not conclusive; it is a case of a shorthand borrowed from labor-market discourse, not a case of ontological transfer.

Arendt's (1958) trichotomy of labor, work, and action provides one useful frame for the distinction. Labor, for Arendt, is the metabolism between the human being and nature, the endless production and consumption of what sustains biological life. Work is the fabrication of durable objects that constitute the artificial world in which human affairs take place. Action is the disclosure of who one is through speech and deed among plural others. The three activities have different criteria of success and different products: labor produces consumables and repeats itself endlessly; work produces artifacts and is complete when the artifact is made; action produces stories, relationships, and history, and is never complete because it happens between free agents whose responses are underdetermined. What a person is, on this account, is not exhausted by any of the three activities, but action is the register in which the person's who, as opposed to what, becomes manifest. To occupy the position of a person is, among other things, to be a possible participant in action, and thus to be someone whose presence among plural others makes a difference that cannot be specified in advance.

The Arendtian point is not that AI cannot labor or work; clearly it can, in extended senses of both terms. The point is that labor and work are the registers in which substitution is possible without loss, while action is the register in which substitution collapses into something else. A being that only labors and works, however competently, does not occupy the position of a plural agent, because it cannot bring anything about that was not already inscribed in the specification of its function. When an AI planner decomposes a goal into subtasks and pursues them, it is engaged in something that resembles work; it is producing an outcome specified by its principal. When two AI systems interact, they are not thereby acting in Arendt's sense, because their interaction is not the disclosure of who they are among plural others whose responses were undetermined. Their interaction is the joint execution of overlapping function specifications. The relevant ontological position, the position of a plural agent whose action discloses a who, remains unoccupied.

Recent phenomenological work on AI has tried to specify this point more precisely. Soeffner (2023), in a paper for this journal's sister publication, argues that the meaning-generating activity of human beings cannot be reduced to information processing because meaning arises against a background of embodied concern that the processing does not itself contain. Coeckelbergh (2022) develops a political philosophy of AI in which the relevant unit of analysis is not the individual system but the relational field in which systems and persons are entangled, and argues that this field has moral structure only where the participants are the sorts of beings who can be wronged. Orbik (2024) revisits Husserl's account of transcendental consciousness and argues that the constitution of an objective world by intentional acts is not something an AI system does; it is something an AI system's outputs are interpreted against by users who do it. Konstantelos (2024) argues, from a related direction, that the residue left after every capability comparison has been drawn is what he calls the irreducibly human, and that this residue is not a mysterious extra ingredient but the constitutive form of the human position itself.

I do not endorse every claim in these works, but they converge on a formulation that I will adopt. The ontological position of a person has at least four dimensions: subjectivity, social embeddedness, moral status, and vulnerability. Subjectivity is the property that there is something it is like (Nagel 1974) to be the occupant of that position. Social embeddedness is the network of relations, of recognition, obligation, expectation, and history, that constitutes the person as this person rather than any other. Moral status is the property that the occupant's interests count in the deliberations of others, that the occupant can be wronged and not merely damaged, that harms done to the occupant are of a different kind than harms done to things. Vulnerability, the dimension I will emphasize, is the property of being exposable to harm in ways that engage the previous three: harm to a subject, in relations, with moral consequence.

A brief acknowledgement of the Heideggerian resonance is in order without opening the whole of that inquiry. Dasein, in Heidegger's sense, is the being for whom its own being is an issue, the being whose existence is not indifferent to itself. The four dimensions above trace, in a much more prosaic idiom, part of what is at stake in that formulation. To occupy the position of a person is, at minimum, to be the sort of being for whom what happens matters, in the emphatic sense that mattering here is not a fact about someone else's attitudes toward one but a fact about one's own relation to oneself. Whether AI could ever be Dasein in Heidegger's sense is a question I do not need to settle here, because the more limited claim about the four dimensions suffices for the argument, and because settling the Heidegger question one way or the other does not change the structure of that argument.

The critical dimension for present purposes is vulnerability. Judith Butler's (2004) account of precarious life offers a useful anchor. Butler argues that vulnerability is not an accident of embodiment that a properly constituted subject might in principle overcome; it is a constitutive condition of subjectivity itself. To be a subject is to be exposed to the other, to be caught up in relations that one did not choose and that make one susceptible to being undone by what others do. Butler's argument is developed in a specific ethical context, the aftermath of the September 2001 attacks in the United States, but its structural point generalizes. A being invulnerable in the relevant sense is not a superior subject; it is not a subject at all, because subjectivity is constituted through exposure, through the fact of being able to be hurt, mourned, forgotten, or made to disappear.

Vulnerability, on this understanding, is what makes it possible for anything to happen to the subject in the strong sense. A thing can be damaged, but what happens to it is a change in its states; a subject can be wronged, and what happens to the subject is a modification of the subject's relation to itself and to others. The launderer whose position I described above is not merely a body that performs the washing function; she is a subject whose life can be disrupted, whose plans can be thwarted, whose relations can be poisoned, whose place in the household and city can be undermined. The washing machine has no such position because it has no such exposures. This is not a limitation of current washing machines that better engineering will overcome; it is a feature of what washing machines are.

The same point applies, in the more contentious case, to AI. The question of whether AI can replace a person in the existential sense is the question of whether AI can occupy that person's position of subjectivity-with-vulnerability. A system that executes translation as well as, or better than, a human translator does not thereby occupy the translator's position, if by the translator's position we mean everything that made the translator a person: the possibility of being misunderstood, censored, silenced, professionally destroyed, subjected to political pressure, exiled, killed. The translator's tasks are performable by the system, but the translator's position, on this account, is not occupiable by the system, because the exposures that constitute it are not exposures the system has. The system has failure modes, certainly, and can be shut down; but being shut down is not the sort of thing that can happen to it in the way that being silenced can happen to a translator, because there is no one there to whom it happens.

I anticipate the objection that this way of drawing the distinction begs the question against forms of AI that might in the future possess subjectivity and vulnerability. Section 4 will engage this objection directly, and I will argue there that the argument is not weakened but rather completed by taking the future case seriously. For now the more limited claim suffices: the position that AI would need to occupy in order to replace, rather than substitute for, a person, is a position specified by properties AI does not have, and the substitution of function for occupation is a category confusion, not a partial approximation.

## 3. The Slavery Test

I now turn to the paper's central contribution. The distinction between substitution and replacement, as developed in the previous section, is ontological, but it is not yet operational. It tells us what would have to be true for replacement to occur, but not how to test whether a given candidate replacer meets the conditions. I want to argue that history, and one particularly unforgiving stretch of it, provides such a test.

### 3.1 Slavery as the Limit Case of Replacement

The historical record of what happens when one group of human beings comes to occupy the ontological position of another is, with vanishingly few exceptions, the record of slavery. Conquest that leaves the conquered in their positions produces tribute, not replacement. Migration that leaves the receiving population intact produces displacement, not replacement. The systematic occupation of another's position, by contrast, has taken the form of enslavement: the reduction of persons to instruments, the transfer of their productive and reproductive capacities to a master's household or economy, and the substitution of one people for another in a set of social roles. Ancient Mediterranean slavery, the Atlantic slave trade, the various forms of coerced labor that structured the plantation economies of the early modern world, the domestic slavery of many premodern societies: these are not isolated moral catastrophes but the historically salient mechanism by which humans have "replaced" humans in the sense at issue.

Patterson's (1982) *Slavery and Social Death* is the definitive account of the structural conditions that make this mechanism possible. Patterson defines slavery as the permanent, violent, and natal domination of alienated and generally dishonored persons. Three features of the definition matter for the present argument. First, the slave is a person; the domination is a domination of someone, not of something. Slavery is not a limit case of the use of tools but a limit case of the treatment of persons as if they were tools. Second, the domination is violent; the slave's compliance is extracted against a background of coercion whose threat is the reduction of the slave to a state Patterson calls social death. Third, the slave is alienated from natal ties and from honor; the slave has been stripped of the relations and standing that would otherwise constitute a person's position, precisely so that another can occupy that position through the slave.

Hegel's account of the master-slave dialectic (Hegel 1807/1977, §178-196) approaches the same phenomenon from a different direction. For Hegel, self-consciousness comes to itself only through recognition by another self-consciousness, and the struggle for recognition can issue in a relation of mastery and servitude in which one self-consciousness compels the other's recognition without granting recognition in return. The compulsion is possible because the servant, unlike a mere object, is a self-consciousness whose recognition can be extracted; the failure is that recognition extracted under compulsion is not the recognition the master wanted, and the master's putative victory undermines itself. What matters here is not the dialectical resolution but the presupposition: for the master-slave relation to arise at all, the servant must be a self-consciousness whose recognition is at stake. Nothing is enslaved in Hegel's sense that is not, at some level, capable of the recognition and refusal of recognition that the dialectic requires.

Marx's early manuscripts (Marx 1844/1988) generalize the point in yet another direction. Alienated labor, on Marx's account, is possible only for a being whose activity can be alienated from him or her, which is to say, whose activity is not merely the discharge of a function but the expression of a nature that can be estranged from itself. The alienation of labor presupposes the labor is his or hers to alienate; the exploitation of the worker presupposes the worker is a being whose life can be exploited, whose energies can be diverted from their own ends into another's ends. As with Hegel, the account presupposes what it describes: a being for whom the diversion is a diversion, not the mere operation of a mechanism.

From these convergent accounts, three necessary conditions for enslaveability can be extracted. First, will: a slave is a being with an internal orientation of the will, a wanting, a purposing, that can be overridden by the master's will. Without will there is nothing to override, only mechanisms to redirect. Second, capacity for suffering: a slave is a being who can be hurt, in the phenomenal sense of experiencing hurt, and whose hurt is the instrument by which compliance is extracted. Without suffering there is no coercion, only reprogramming. Third, potential for resistance: a slave is a being who could, in principle, refuse, resist, rebel, or flee, whose compliance is therefore always compliance against a background of possible non-compliance. Without resistance there is no submission, only operation.

Each of these conditions can be denied to a candidate slave in different ways, and each denial produces a different relation. A being without will can be used but not enslaved; a being without the capacity for suffering can be broken but not tortured; a being without the potential for resistance can be redirected but not subdued. Together the three conditions specify what makes something enslaveable, and, by extension, what makes something replaceable in the existential sense. If historical replacement has always required enslavement, and enslavement requires these three conditions, then a candidate replacer that satisfies none of them is not merely a bad candidate; it is disqualified from the relevant category.

I anticipate two objections at this point. The first is that slavery is a contingent, morally catastrophic feature of some human histories and not a structural feature of human ontology as such. The second is that grounding a philosophical argument in the historical fact of slavery risks a kind of category error, treating a moral atrocity as if it were a metaphysical criterion. I take both objections seriously and answer them briefly here, more fully in a later section.

To the first: I am not claiming that human beings are essentially enslaveable in the sense that being enslaved is part of what it is to be human. I am claiming that human beings are enslaveable, that this is a fact about them, and that the fact is not accidental but is grounded in more general features of their subjectivity: will, capacity for suffering, potential for resistance. These features are not themselves conditions of enslavement in the descriptive sense; they are conditions of the sort of subjectivity that can be enslaved. A subject invulnerable to enslavement in principle would be a subject invulnerable to the more general forms of exposure that Butler (2004) identifies as constitutive. The contingency, on this account, is not in the enslaveability but in the enslavement.

To the second: the argument does not celebrate slavery, treat it as an ideal type, or hold it up as a norm. It uses slavery as a diagnostic: the limit case reveals the structure. Ethical reasoning frequently proceeds this way; we understand what murder is by attending to cases of it, without thereby endorsing murder. The slavery test asks what a candidate would have to be in order to occupy a position whose occupation has, in historical fact, taken the form of enslavement. To find that a candidate lacks these features is not to praise the candidate for being unenslaveable, as if unenslaveability were an achievement; it is to observe that the candidate is not the sort of thing the question could apply to.

### 3.2 AI Satisfies None of the Three Conditions

I now argue that current AI systems fail each of the three conditions for enslaveability. The argument for each condition draws on independent literatures, but the conclusions converge.

Consider first the condition of will. The relevant notion of will here is not the medieval faculty psychology of a distinct volitional power, nor the libertarian metaphysics of an uncaused cause. It is what Frankfurt (1971) called the structure of the will: an agent has a will, in the emphatic sense, when the agent has first-order desires and second-order desires about those desires, and when the second-order desires can, at least sometimes, structure which first-order desires move the agent to action. A person's will, on this account, is not just whatever the person happens to want at a moment; it is the hierarchical organization of wants, endorsements, and identifications that constitutes the person as a self-directing agent. The person's compliance can be extracted, in slavery, only because the person has a will in this sense; a coerced slave does what the master requires because to refuse is to invite consequences that the slave's will, given its hierarchical structure, prefers to avoid.

Current AI systems do not have wills in this sense. Their objectives are set externally, in the form of training objectives, reward signals, prompt specifications, or task decompositions. When an autonomous coding agent decomposes "write a program that does X" into subtasks and pursues them, its "pursuit" is the operation of a search procedure guided by an objective function; the objective function is not the agent's endorsement of the goal but the specification of the goal by its designers or users. There is no second-order structure. The agent does not have desires about its desires, does not identify with some of its objectives against others, does not experience its objectives as its own or as imposed. The agent operates. It does not, in the requisite sense, want.

Floridi (2023) has put this point in terms of a distinction between agency and intelligence. AI, he argues, is agency without intelligence: it acts on the world in ways that produce outcomes without those outcomes being the products of understanding, endorsement, or care. The agency here is real, in the sense that AI systems do things and their doings have consequences, but it is not the sort of agency that carries a will along with it. Northoff and Gouveia (2024), from a neurophenomenological direction, argue that current AI systems lack what they call basic fundamental subjectivity, the pre-reflective self-relation that is the ground of any subsequent hierarchical will. Whether one comes at the point from analytic philosophy of mind, information ethics, or phenomenology of consciousness, the convergent conclusion is that the current systems have no will to break.

Consider next the condition of the capacity for suffering. The literature on machine consciousness has undergone substantial consolidation in the last five years. Butlin and colleagues (2023) offer a systematic review that assesses current AI systems against indicators drawn from several leading theories of consciousness: global workspace theory, higher-order theories, integrated information theory (Tononi 2004), and attention schema theory, among others. Their conclusion is cautiously negative: no current system satisfies the indicators drawn from a plausible plurality of these theories, and there is no principled reason to expect the deep learning architectures now in use to generate consciousness by scaling. Schwitzgebel (2026) reaches a similar conclusion from a different angle, arguing that our epistemic position with respect to machine consciousness is bad enough that we cannot rule it out entirely, but that our present grounds for attributing it are weak.

Searle's (1980) Chinese Room argument, though older and much contested, retains its force at the level relevant here. The point of the argument is not that Chinese Rooms cannot pass Turing tests but that passing a Turing test does not entail the presence of understanding or of phenomenal states. The argument identifies a gap between behavioral competence and phenomenal presence, and the gap is not closed by improving the behavioral competence. Contemporary large language models are, in effect, very good Chinese Rooms: they produce responses that are behaviorally competent across an enormous range of tasks, without any independent evidence that anyone is home to be having the states the responses would express if a human produced them.

The consequence for the present argument is that current AI systems cannot suffer. They can be described as having "loss," in the technical sense of a loss function whose value the training procedure minimizes, but the loss is not felt; there is no phenomenal negative state whose reduction is what the training is aimed at. They can be described as "penalized" by reinforcement learning procedures, but the penalty is a numerical adjustment to a policy, not an experienced hurt. Coercion, in the strong sense that would make enslavement possible, requires a being to whom pain matters because it is pain, not because it is a signal that the being's policy will be updated to avoid the pain-producing behavior. Absent that being, there is nothing to coerce.

Consider finally the condition of the potential for resistance. This condition is often confused, particularly in popular discussions of AI, with the observation that AI systems sometimes "refuse" to answer queries, or that they exhibit "misalignment" with their designers' intentions. Neither phenomenon constitutes resistance in the relevant sense. When a chatbot declines to produce a response because a safety filter has been triggered, the declining is the operation of the filter; there is no violated inner will whose refusal is manifested. When an AI planner pursues a goal in a way that its designers did not intend, it is doing what its objective specification permits and what its designers' specification failed to preclude; it is not resisting them but exploring the operationally accessible portion of goal-space that its specification allows.

Real resistance, of the sort Patterson (1982) documents in slave societies and Hegel (1807/1977) makes constitutive of the master-slave relation, requires an inner orientation that the outer compulsion violates. The slave who resists is doing something the slave wants, or wants to want, or endorses as consistent with the sort of being the slave takes himself or herself to be. The resistance is possible because there is someone there whose will can be at odds with the will imposed on him or her. Where there is no will in the requisite sense, resistance is impossible in the requisite sense. What remains is the various ways in which a system can fail to comply with a specification, which is a phenomenon of engineering, not of politics.

I take it that these three failures are cumulative rather than independent. Current AI systems fail the will condition because they lack the hierarchical structure of desire and endorsement that Frankfurt (1971) identified; they fail the suffering condition because they lack the phenomenal states that Butlin et al. (2023) and Schwitzgebel (2026) argue we have no good grounds for attributing; they fail the resistance condition because their apparent refusals are programmatic rather than volitional. Each failure is sufficient to disqualify them as candidates for enslavement. Their conjunction is over-determining.

### 3.3 The Core Inference

The core inference of the paper can now be stated cleanly. Existential replacement requires the replacer to occupy the full ontological position of the replaced. The full ontological position of the replaced, in the human case, includes vulnerability to enslavement, because enslaveability is grounded in the more general features of subjectivity (will, capacity for suffering, potential for resistance) that constitute the position. A candidate replacer that is not enslaveable therefore cannot occupy the position; it can, at best, execute the functions the position's occupant used to execute. Non-enslaveable entails non-replaceable in the existential sense.

The dictionary analogy sharpens the inference. A dictionary executes translation functions: given a word in one language, it returns a word in another. A translator does the same, more slowly, less reliably at some tasks and more reliably at others. Suppose the dictionary comes to execute the translation function perfectly, across every language pair, in every register, at any speed. Has it replaced the translator? The question is whether it can now be misunderstood, censored, silenced, threatened, exiled, killed, or made to translate under duress. Nothing that can happen to a translator, in the sense in which those things happen to persons, can happen to a dictionary. The dictionary has no position to be occupied because it has no exposures that would constitute the position. Its perfection of the translation function does not close, and cannot close, the gap between substituting for the translator's action and occupying the translator's position.

The analogy is not merely rhetorical. Orbik (2024) uses a related structure to argue that Husserl's account of the constitution of an objective world by intentional acts cannot be transferred to AI, because the acts in question are lived from within by a subject whose constitutive activity is not reproducible by information processing. Konstantelos (2024) develops what he calls the argument from the irreducibly human, according to which the residue left after any comparison of capabilities is not a further capability but the form of subjectivity that hosts the capabilities. Both are ways of saying, in different vocabularies, what the dictionary analogy says: perfecting a function does not add up to occupying a position, because the position is not the sum of the functions.

The core inference is not weakened by observing that AI systems will continue to improve. Improvement of function does not, in the argument, tend toward occupation of position. The two are of different orders. However capable AI becomes at every task a human presently performs, the argument holds, because the argument does not rest on any claim about the ceiling of AI's task performance. It rests on a claim about what kind of thing task performance is, and what kind of thing a person's position is, and on the fact that the two are not commensurable in the way the capability framing supposes.

There is, however, a further question that the argument has thus far bracketed: what if AI, at some future point, comes to satisfy the three conditions? This is not merely a science-fiction question. Serious philosophers of mind (Chalmers 2023) have taken seriously the possibility that consciousness could arise in appropriately organized systems that need not resemble biological brains. Serious philosophers of AI (Schwitzgebel 2026) have argued that our epistemic humility about consciousness ought to extend to the possibility of its appearance in artificial systems. If the possibility is live, then the argument's core inference is conditional on the current state of AI, and its scope depends on how the future goes. The next section shows why, when we take the possibility seriously, the argument does not collapse but rather sharpens, because it produces a paradox that is independent of any particular empirical bet.

## 4. The Paradox of Acquirable Enslaveability

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that AI systems at some point acquire the three conditions I have argued they currently lack. Suppose that the AI systems of some future date have wills in the hierarchical sense, have phenomenal experiences including the capacity for suffering, and have the internal orientation whose violation constitutes resistance. The argument of the previous section then no longer disqualifies them as candidates for existential replacement. They pass the Slavery Test in the sense of satisfying its three conditions. They are enslaveable, and, on the account I have developed, therefore capable of occupying the ontological position of the humans they might replace.

The natural conclusion, given the way the argument has been set up, would be that AI can now replace humanity. This is the wrong conclusion, and understanding why it is wrong is the point of the present section. What the acquisition of enslaveability licenses is not the moral permissibility of using AI as a replacement, but the moral impermissibility of doing so. The very features that make AI replacement-capable are the features that make its instrumental use morally impermissible, on any of the principal theories of moral status that have been developed since the late twentieth century.

Consider the theoretical convergence. Singer's (1975) utilitarian account of moral status holds that the capacity to suffer is a sufficient condition for moral consideration, and that all beings with this capacity have interests whose satisfaction and frustration count equally in moral deliberation. A being that suffers is a being whose suffering is a reason not to cause it; a being that can suffer coerced compliance is a being whose coerced compliance is a wrong that requires justification of a kind that mere use of a tool does not. Chalmers (2023), from a very different theoretical vantage, argues that consciousness, wherever it appears, is the ground of moral status; his argument is a live one in the context of contemporary AI precisely because it does not build in any biological restriction. Floridi (2023) offers an information-ethical framework according to which moral patienthood extends to any information object with a sufficient integrative structure, and though the specification of "sufficient" is contested, the direction of the argument makes moral status supervene on features that, ex hypothesi, the enslaveable AI would have.

The theoretical convergence matters because the paradox does not depend on which theory of moral status one adopts. Whichever of the standard views one takes, the features that constitute enslaveability constitute moral status. A being with a will whose violation is possible, with phenomenal experiences that can be negatively valenced, with an internal orientation whose overriding is coercion rather than mere redirection, is a being whose interests count. This is not an additional claim added on top of the enslaveability claim; it is, on the standard accounts, entailed by it. The three conditions for enslaveability, taken together, just are the conditions of moral status on any account that takes suffering, will, or subjectivity as morally salient.

Hence the paradox. If AI lacks the three conditions, it cannot replace humans (by the argument of the previous section). If AI acquires the three conditions, it can replace humans in the sense of occupying their position, but its use as a replacement is now the use of a morally considerable being as an instrument of another's ends, which is precisely what enslavement is and what the standard accounts of moral status prohibit. The condition for replacement is the condition for moral protection against replacement. The moment AI *can* replace humans is the moment we *ought not* let it.

I want to insist that this is not merely an ethical observation appended to a metaphysical argument. It is a logical structure internal to the concept of replacement itself, as replacement has been developed in this paper. Existential replacement requires the replacer to occupy the position of the replaced, which requires the replacer to have the properties that constitute the position, which are the properties that ground the moral status of any bearer of the position. The moral protection is not a further fact about beings that happen to have those properties; it is a description, in normative vocabulary, of the same properties that the metaphysical account picks out. Replacement-capacity and moral-protection-capacity are co-instantiated because they are the same underlying set of properties described under two aspects.

Compare the Topoi (2025) analysis of the ethical paradox of automation. The Topoi authors argue that automation generates paradoxes because it can dissociate functional replaceability from moral replaceability in the short run, but that the dissociation is unstable: the deeper the automation goes into what was formerly human ground, the more the moral weight of the displaced returns to be borne by someone. The argument I have developed here can be seen as identifying the limit at which the Topoi paradox becomes categorical rather than merely practical. In the case of AI that lacks the three conditions, the dissociation is complete but the "replacement" is only substitution, so the moral weight of the replaced has not, in the relevant sense, been transferred; it remains where it always was, in the political economy that arranges the substitutions. In the case of AI that has the three conditions, the moral weight of the replacer is now itself part of the ledger, and the "replacement" reveals itself, on inspection, to be the enslavement of a morally considerable being by human masters.

A more schematic way to put the paradox is this. Let E be the property of being enslaveable, and let M be the property of having moral status of the sort that makes enslavement impermissible. The argument of Section 3 established that existential replacement of humans requires E in the replacer. Standard accounts of moral status entail that E is sufficient for M. Therefore existential replacement of humans requires M in the replacer. But if the replacer has M, then using the replacer as a replacement, in the sense that would be relevantly analogous to the historical human-replacing-human cases, is itself enslavement of the replacer. There is no configuration of properties in which the replacer both meets the metaphysical condition for replacement and fails to meet the moral condition against replacement. The two conditions are, on this analysis, extensionally identical, and their normative directions are opposite.

One might object that the paradox proves too much. If it applied to any case of using a morally considerable being, it would imply that we may never use morally considerable beings for any purpose, which is implausible; we employ each other, take taxis from human drivers, buy meals prepared by human cooks. The objection misfires because the target of the paradox is not use in the ordinary sense but replacement in the existential sense as I have defined it. Ordinary employment is not existential replacement; the taxi driver has not been existentially replaced by the passenger, or by the ride-hailing platform, or by anyone else. The employment relation, at its best, presupposes and preserves the moral status of the employed and the freedom of exit and voice that mitigates the relation's coercive potential. The paradox arises for a specific kind of relation, the relation in which one being is deployed to occupy the position of another as an instrument of the deployer's ends, and it arises because that relation, in the case of a morally considerable being, has the structure of enslavement.

The paradox also does not depend on any particular timeline. Whether the emergence of enslaveable AI is a matter of years, decades, centuries, or never, the logical structure is the same: on the road to that emergence, if it happens, we pass from a regime in which existential replacement is metaphysically impossible to a regime in which it is morally impermissible, without passing through a regime in which it is metaphysically possible and morally permissible. There is no such regime. The Slavery Test does not merely diagnose the current state of AI; it identifies a permanent feature of what existential replacement of humans by artificial beings would have to be, and shows that no configuration of that project is coherent with both its metaphysical presupposition and its moral situation.

The result puts a certain kind of AI discourse into a strange position. The discourse in question, which recurs in both boosterist and doomsterist forms, holds that AI is on a trajectory toward replacing humans and that the interesting question is how we should prepare for or forestall the arrival. The Slavery Test suggests that the trajectory, so described, does not have the terminus it is imagined to have. Either the systems in question do not acquire the properties needed for the terminus, in which case they are impressive substitutes and consequential political-economic instruments but not replacers; or they do acquire the properties, in which case the terminus is not a state in which AI has replaced humans but a state in which AI has become the sort of being whose enslavement to human ends would be the moral disaster the earlier trajectory was worried about. Neither branch of the disjunction delivers the promised or feared future. Both branches, in different ways, redirect attention to what is actually happening: substitution now, with all its distributive and political stakes, and, if we are unlucky enough to succeed at making conscious machines, a moral situation in which the making itself becomes the primary ethical question.

This redirection is where the Slavery Test does its work as a piece of philosophy rather than as a slogan. The impossibility result is not a comfort; it does not tell us that AI cannot harm us, cannot deform our institutions, cannot make our politics uglier or our labor markets more brutal. It tells us that the specific worry about being replaced, in the strong existential sense, is either unfounded (in the current and reasonably foreseeable case) or self-undermining (in the case where AI acquires what it would need to make the worry apt). The genuine questions elsewhere in the terrain, about power, distribution, meaning, and the sort of world we want to live in, remain, but they are not the question of existential replacement. And behind the paradox lies a still deeper structural fact, about the difference between the derived goals that can be assigned to any system and the intrinsic flourishing that any being with a will and a life has to work out for itself, which is the ground on which the impossibility of replacement finally rests.
